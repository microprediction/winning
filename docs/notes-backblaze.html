Disks do not fail independently

A common-cause factor in the public Backblaze data, and what it does to durability math. September 2026.

Storage reliability rests on an assumption that is almost always left implicit: that drives fail independently. Mean time between failures, the annualized failure rate, and the durability guarantees of erasure coding all multiply per-drive probabilities as if the events were unrelated. This note tests the assumption on Backblaze's public drive-failure data and finds it false in the way that matters most for data loss — and the correlated alternative is exactly what this package computes.

A request that fans out and waits for the slowest backend is the maximum of a correlated race; a stored dataset that survives until the k-th of its drives fails is a correlated order statistic. The engine here prices both. Reliability is the min-wins side of it, and the classical competing-risks formula for which cause strikes first is one of its special cases. So the question is empirical: in a real fleet, is the correlation large enough to matter?

The data

Backblaze publishes, openly, one record per drive per day: model, a failure flag, and SMART attributes, from 2013 to the present. We use the first quarter of 2025 — about 340,000 drives, 90 days, 1,067 failures across 76 models — reduced to the daily failure count of each model. The drive model is the correlation variable we can see: drives of the same model share a manufacturing lineage and firmware. What Backblaze does not publish is rack or datacenter placement, so the finer common cause of a shared power or cooling domain is invisible; the correlation found below is therefore a floor, not a ceiling.

Independence is falsified, and it is not aging

Under independent constant-hazard failure, a cohort's daily failure count is Poisson, with its variance equal to its mean. The fleet's daily count is over-dispersed by a factor of 3.16. The obvious objection is that this is drift, not correlation: cohorts age and turn over across a quarter, and a rising baseline inflates the variance. But aging is a slow trend and cannot make different drives fail on the same calendar day. Removing a smooth seven-day per-drive hazard trend and adjusting for cohort size leaves the dispersion at 2.88 — almost all of it is genuine same-day clustering that no trend explains.

The clustering is visible as discrete events. Six days in the quarter carry more than three standard deviations of excess failures, roughly double the expected count, and each of those days spans between seven and thirteen distinct drive models:

A bad manufacturing batch would concentrate in one model; an event that touches every manufacturer at once points instead to something they share. What they share, though, is not only their environment — it is also the process that records their failures, and that is the confound this note takes seriously below.

The test aging cannot fake

The decisive check separates two manufacturers whose drives fail by unrelated mechanisms. After removing each manufacturer's own trend, do their daily failure residuals still move together? They do, and positively:

Seagate and HGST have different platters, heads, firmware and failure physics. Their detrended failures co-moving on a daily basis has only one explanation: a factor shared by the drives regardless of who made them, which is to say the environment they sit in. That is the common cause a durability model needs, and the one an independent MTBF calculation cannot see.

The effect replicates. A second quarter (the fourth of 2024) shows the same signature — overdispersion surviving detrend, and positive same-day correlation across every manufacturer pair — though weaker, with the Seagate–HGST figure at +0.21 rather than +0.40. The direction is robust across both quarters; the magnitude varies, so the numbers here are a high-correlation quarter, not a universal constant. One tempting explanation can be ruled out: it is not heat. Datacenter drive temperature barely moves (a couple of degrees around 33°C), does not correlate with the daily failure residual, and removing it leaves the cross-manufacturer correlation untouched. Whatever the shared factor is — power, maintenance, handling, humidity — it is not the thermometer, and pinning it down would need the placement and operations data the public release omits.

The uncomfortable alternative

Before reading a physical cause into the clustering, consider a duller one. Backblaze's failure date is the day a drive is marked failed, which need not be the day it physically died: if drives are pulled and recorded in batches on a maintenance cadence, every manufacturer will appear to spike on the same administrative days, with no shared physics at all. The data bears this out in part. There is a weekday signature — failures run about 15% higher on weekdays than weekends, and the first quarter is Friday-heavy, with half its spike days falling on Fridays. Removing the day-of-week pattern cuts the cross-manufacturer correlation from +0.19 to +0.12: roughly a third of it is the weekly calendar, and two thirds survives. But day-of-week removal only catches the weekly part; irregular bulk-removal batching would survive it and is indistinguishable, from failure dates alone, from drives genuinely failing together.

This matters most for the durability reading below. That argument needs the co-timing to be physical — drives actually failing together, which is what threatens erasure-coded data. If the co-timing is administrative — drives failing on different real days but recorded together — durability is untouched. Failure dates cannot tell the two apart, and the weekday test shows at least some of the clustering is administrative. So take the next section as conditional: it is what the correlation costs if it is physical, an upper bound the data cannot confirm.

Does it predict, or just describe?

A correlation seen in hindsight is worth little if it does not transfer. It does. Fitting a single overdispersion parameter on the fourth quarter of 2024 and carrying it, unchanged, to the first quarter of 2025, the correlated model predicts the held-out daily failure counts with 13% lower log-loss than the independent calculation — and on the heaviest-failure days, the top decile that erasure coding exists to survive, 37% lower. The independent model is not merely optimistic; it is most wrong exactly where being wrong costs data. One number, learned on one quarter, measurably improves the next.

What it costs, in durability

Erasure-coded storage loses data when the k-th of its drives fails before repair — a k-out-of-n order statistic, not a single failure. Common cause makes several drives fail together, so it is precisely the tail that erasure coding is designed to protect. Fitting a single shared daily factor to the fleet (a modest loading, well short of the worst individual models) and pricing the probability of k simultaneous failures among twenty correlated drives, against the independent calculation:

The independent calculation under-states the risk of correlated data loss by up to twentyfold at the depth that matters, and by more within the individual models that carry the strongest common cause. The engine computes the exact value; the point is that it is nowhere near the independent one.

Two systems races, one wrong assumption in opposite directions. For the maximum of a fan-out — tail latency — positive correlation makes the tail lighter, so the independence formula over-provisions capacity. For the count of failures, common cause makes the tail heavier, so independence under-states durability risk. Both are the same missing factor; the engine prices both.

What this does and does not show

It shows that in a real, public fleet the failure correlation is large enough to change durability math, that it is environmental rather than a batch artifact, and that it is visible without any private telemetry. It does not resolve the finer structure — a rack losing power, a hot aisle — because the public data carries no placement, so the factor measured here is fleet-wide and the true correlation within a failure domain is larger. The baseline is a constant-hazard null; a survival model with drive age would sharpen the numbers without changing the direction. Every figure above comes from a committed script.
