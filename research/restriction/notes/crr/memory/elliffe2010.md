# Elliffe & Davison (2010)

## Citation

Elliffe, D., & Davison, M. (2010). Four-alternative choice violates the constant-ratio rule.
*Behavioural Processes*, 84(1), 381–389. DOI 10.1016/j.beproc.2009.11.009. PMID 19941942.
Available online 2009 (hence often cited as 2009); print 2010. University of Auckland.

## Domain and stimuli

**Operant choice in pigeons** — concurrent variable-interval schedules, four keys. Not
speech, not letters, not memory: this is free-operant preference, the matching-law tradition.
Included because it is the only paper in the sweep whose title flatly asserts a CRR
violation, and because the matching literature calls Luce's proportional-renormalization
assumption by exactly the same name.

Six pigeons. Four-key concurrent VI schedule. A 27:9:3:1 distribution of reinforcers across
the four keys, with the assignment of that distribution to physical keys **changing every 10
reinforcers** — a rapidly-changing procedure, so behaviour must track the ratio within
session rather than settle at asymptote.

## Master response set and restricted response sets (nested, overlapping, or a relabelling)

Nested by extraction rather than by menu manipulation, and this is the important structural
point. All four alternatives are continuously available; nothing is ever removed from the
pigeon's menu. The CRR test is performed by taking **pairwise preference between two of the
four keys** and asking whether it depends only on the relative reinforcer rates of those two
keys — which is precisely the constant-ratio / IIA claim — or also on the absolute rates,
i.e. on what the *other* two alternatives are doing.

It fails. Preference between a pair depended "not only on the relative reinforcer rates on
those keys, but also on the absolute levels of those rates."

So this is a **context-dependence-of-surviving-odds** test conducted inside a fixed
four-alternative set, rather than a master-matrix / submatrix test across menus. For this
project that is arguably the cleaner logical form: there is no confound from the act of
removing an option, because nothing is removed. The odds between two survivors simply are not
invariant to the rest of the choice set.

Companion result, essential context: **Bensemann, Lobb, Podlesnik & Elliffe (2015)**, JEAB
104, 7–19, ran the steady-state version of the same experiment — same six-pigeon,
four-alternative, 27:9:3:1 design, but with each schedule assignment held for 50 sessions —
and found the CRR **satisfied**. See bensemann2015.md. The pair together localise the
violation to rapidly-changing environments, i.e. to the regime where the animal is still
inferring the structure. That is a strong parallel to a model answering under uncertainty
about what the menu means, and it is worth stating carefully rather than citing Elliffe &
Davison alone.

## What numbers are printed (which tables, counts or proportions, per subject or pooled)

Not verified beyond the abstract. What the abstract establishes is present, in kind:

- Extended (session-level) preference measures as a function of the four-way reinforcer ratio.
- **Local preference pulses** — transient increases in responding on the just-reinforced key,
  resolved as a function of position within the 10-reinforcer component and of whether the
  reinforcer came from a rich or lean key.
- **Switching patterns** between keys, graded by the reinforcer rates on each key.
- Model comparison against a **contingency-discriminability model** that assumes organisms
  "may occasionally misattribute reinforcers to a response that did not produce them."

No confusion matrices in the identification sense. The data are response and reinforcer
counts per key per component, per pigeon; JEAB/Behavioural Processes papers from this group
typically print per-subject parameter tables and pooled figures. Six subjects, so per-animal
data are likely tabulated.

## Usability verdict (usable now / needs digitizing / needs library access / unusable, and why)

**Needs library access** for the numbers; **usable now** as a citation, since the abstract
alone states the result unambiguously and is quotable.

Judgement on whether to chase it: **medium priority, and cite it with care.** The abstract is
enough to make the argumentative point, and the raw material is pigeon key-peck counts, which
will not transfer to anything in this project's data. The reason to get the full text is not
the data but the modelling: the contingency-discriminability account — that the violation
arises because the organism sometimes **misattributes a reinforcer to a response that did not
produce it** — is a concrete generative mechanism for CRR failure, and it is a
confusion-between-alternatives mechanism, which is exactly the Thurstonian-noise family
rather than the renormalization family.

Caveat to carry into any citation: the effect is procedure-specific. Bensemann et al. (2015)
obtained CRR adherence with the identical design run to steady state. Citing "four-alternative
choice violates the constant-ratio rule" without that qualification would misrepresent the
literature.

## What the authors concluded, quoted verbatim where possible

Full abstract, verbatim:

> "Six pigeons responded on a four-key concurrent variable-interval schedule in which a
> 27:9:3:1 distribution of reinforcers between the keys changed every 10 reinforcers. Their
> behaviour quickly came under the control of this changing four-way reinforcer ratio.
> However, preference between a pair of keys depended not only on the relative reinforcer
> rates on those keys, but also on the absolute levels of those rates. This contradicts the
> constant-ratio rule that underpins the matching approach to choice, but is predicted by a
> contingency-discriminability model that assumes that organisms may occasionally misattribute
> reinforcers to a response that did not produce them. Reinforcers produced strong preference
> pulses, or transient increases in responding on the just-reinforced key. Despite accurate
> tracking of the reinforcer ratio, reinforcers obtained late in components and from leaner
> keys still produced strong pulses, suggesting both extended and local control of behaviour.
> Patterns of switching between keys were graded and similarly controlled by the reinforcer
> rates on each key. Whether considered in terms of switching, local preference pulses, or
> extended preference, behaviour was controlled by a rapidly changing four-way reinforcer
> ratio in a graduated, continuous manner that is unlikely to be explained by a simple
> heuristic such as fix-and-sample."

The operative sentence is the third and fourth: "preference between a pair of keys depended
not only on the relative reinforcer rates on those keys, but also on the absolute levels of
those rates. This contradicts the constant-ratio rule that underpins the matching approach to
choice."
