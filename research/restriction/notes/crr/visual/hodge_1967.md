# Hodge (1967)

## Citation

Hodge, M. H. (1967). Some further tests of the constant-ratio rule.
*Perception & Psychophysics*, 2(10), 429–437.
doi:10.3758/BF03208790
(University of Georgia. Supported by Contract AF 19(604)-7299 between the USAF Electronic
Systems Division, Bedford MA, and the University of Georgia. Accepted 24 May 1967.)

## Stimuli and master response set

**Five experiments**, two kinesthetic and (for our purposes) **two visual**, plus a
re-tabulation of Hodge & Pollack's (1962) auditory data for comparison. From the abstract:

> "Five experiments sought to test the constant-ratio rule (CRR) with single dimensional
> ensembles composed of 2, 4, or 8 stimulus objects. Each S attempted to identify stimuli
> which varied in weight or in visual size or brightness."

All ensembles are **unidimensional**, which is the point of the paper — Clarke had argued
in correspondence that CRR must fail for ordered unidimensional stimuli.

**Visual Experiment 1 — Visual Area.** Eight gray circles varying in total area, projected
on a screen (0.5 s exposure, 6 s cycle, 4 s response window, responses marked on
mark-sense cards). Four *series* A/B/C/D differing in interstimulus spacing and hence
ensemble range: area increments 1.55, 3.10, 4.65, 6.20 in² respectively; stimulus 1 was
50.24 in² in every series. Ranges of B, C, D are 2×, 3×, 4× that of A. Table 2 is a
schematic showing which physical circles are shared between series — e.g. pair D1-2 is
physically the same pair of circles as B1-3 and as A1-5. 40 subjects, 11/10/10/9 per
series, 2 h/day for six days. Response = speaking/marking the numeral 1–8.

**Visual Experiment 2 — Visual Brightness.** Eight circles varying in brightness, Munsell
neutral tints N 5.5/ to N 9.0/ in half steps, photographed against Stimulus 1 of
Experiment 1 as background, mounted as 2×2 slides; same apparatus. 12 subjects, 1 h/day for
11 days, 12 practice trials on the 8×8 matrix, 576 responses per stimulus per ensemble.

**Lifted Weights Experiments 1 and 2** (kinesthetic, included for completeness): eight
sand-filled medicine bottles on a turntable, blindfolded S, 150–206 g in 8 g steps (Exp 1)
or 6 g steps (Exp 2); numeral responses 1–8. 12 subjects total.

**Master response set = the 8-object ensemble** in every experiment, with numerals 1–8 as
the response labels.

## Restricted response sets (nested, overlapping, or a relabelling)

**Nested subsets of sizes 4 and 2, drawn from the 8-object master, run as separate
randomly-ordered sequences — and additionally a deliberate RELABELLING manipulation.**

Visual Experiment 1 (area): after 16 practice trials on the 8-object ensemble, each S
received "32 sequences of 96 items. The 32 sequences consisted of four sequences of 8
objects, two sequences each of 7 ensembles of 4 objects, and one sequence each of 14
ensembles of 2 objects." Per stimulus per ensemble, summed over sequences and subjects:
528 presentations in series A, 480 in B and C, 432 in D.

Lifted Weights Exp 1: "each S judged four sequences of 8 objects, two sequences each of 8
ensembles of 4 objects, and one sequence each of 14 ensembles of 2 objects" — 384
presentations per stimulus per ensemble. Exp 2: 6 ensembles of 4 and 12 ensembles of 2,
192 presentations.

Ensemble order was random, "i.e., any given sequence could contain 8, 4, or 2 stimulus
objects," and "With the presentation of each new ensemble, S lifted the appropriate bottles
and was told their identity" — so the restricted response set was explicitly announced. No
feedback during experimental sequences.

**The relabelling manipulation.** Because the four spacing series share physical stimulus
pairs while assigning them *different numerals*, Hodge can compare the same physical pair
under different response labels. Table 4 exploits this. He concludes the labelling effect is
weak:

> "To be noted first is the influence of the 'label' assigned in the 2 by 2 tests upon the
> observed confusability of a given pair (compare the entries within parentheses across a
> given line in Table 4). It is apparent that the observed confusabilities of certain pairs
> (e.g., those with a relative spacing of 0-6, 1-4, and 0-3) tend to depend upon the labels
> assigned to the circles. However, since other pairs (e.g., 0-12, 0-8, and 0-2) fail to
> show this dependency, a systematic relationship between labels and observed
> confusability is doubtful."

A **practice** manipulation is layered on: the 16 practice trials on the 8×8 matrix were
grouped in blocks of four and each block used to predict the 4×4 and 2×2 experimental
matrices, giving four sets of CRR predictions from four successively better-practised
master matrices. A control with 17 extra subjects given 4, 8 or 12 trials on the 8×8 before
the subset tests was run to rule out transfer confounding.

## What numbers are printed (which tables, counts or proportions, per subject or pooled)

**This is the fatal problem: no confusion matrix is printed anywhere in the paper.** Only
summary difference measures and scatterplots.

Everything is **pooled over subjects**, deliberately:

> "The CRR and Luce's choice theory are explicitly concerned with the responses of
> individual Ss, but Clarke (1957) and Clarke and Anderson (1962) have shown that grouped
> data provide satisfactory tests of the rule. Grouped data were employed in all
> calculations of the present experiments."

What is printed:

- **Table 1** — summary for the two Lifted Weights experiments only: matrix size (8×8, 4×4,
  2×2, each split into "1st half"/"2nd half" by rank-ordered proportion correct), mean
  proportion correct, mean absolute obtained–predicted difference, % of |differences| > .10,
  and the **mean algebraic (signed) difference on the diagonal only**.
- **Table 2** — the schematic of stimulus spacing across series A–D (no response data).
- **Table 3** — "Summary of Results of Single and Multidimensional Experiments": the same
  four measures, for 4×4 and 2×2 separately, for auditory duration and intensity, auditory
  intensity-spacing A–D, **visual brightness**, **visual area spacing A–D**, lifted weights
  6 g and 8 g, and multidimensional auditory. For the visual rows, 4×4:
  brightness .871 / .027 / 6.2 / +.037; area A .579 / .037 / 7.1 / −.005;
  B .692 / .031 / 3.6 / −.010; C .714 / .042 / 8.0 / −.001; D .781 / .026 / 2.7 / +.015.
  2×2: brightness .976 / .035 / 17.9 / +.022; area A .865 / .062 / 32.1 / +.042;
  B .889 / .034 / 7.1 / −.001; C .930 / .039 / 17.9 / +.035; D .945 / .037 / 14.3 / +.032.
- **Table 4** — "Obtained (in parentheses) and Predicted Confusability Scores for Selected
  Stimulus Pairs in Experiment 1": for selected pairs only, the CRR-derived confusability
  from the 8×8 and the observed confusability from the 2×2, per series. A few dozen numbers,
  not a matrix.
- **Figures 1 and 3** — scatterplots of obtained (ordinate) against CRR-predicted (abscissa)
  proportions for *all individual cells* of all 4×4 and 2×2 matrices (Fig 1 = Lifted Weights
  Exp 1; Fig 3 = Visual Area series A). These contain the cell-level data but as an
  undifferentiated cloud of dots with no cell identity and no readable master matrix.
- **Figure 2** — confusability as a function of interstimulus spacing.

The full matrices were deposited with the journal, not published (footnote 3):

> "Tables, similar to Table 1 and which summarize the data of all the visual experiments,
> have been deposited in the office of this Journal. Copies are available free upon request.
> A portion of this information is presented in Table 3."

Note that even the deposit is described as *summary* tables "similar to Table 1", not raw
matrices.

## Usability verdict (usable now / needs digitizing / needs library access / unusable, and why)

**UNUSABLE for a zero-parameter forecasting contest, despite having exactly the right
experimental design.** This is the most frustrating item in the literature.

Why it fails:
1. **No master matrix is printed.** Without the 8×8 cell values there is nothing to
   renormalise, so no CRR (or Thurstone) forecast can be constructed at all. The paper
   reports only *differences between* obtained and predicted, never the predictions or the
   obtaineds themselves.
2. **Everything is pooled over subjects** (40 subjects in Visual Exp 1), so even if the
   matrices surfaced they would carry the pooling artefact that Morgan (1974) showed
   inflates apparent CRR success.
3. **The deposited tables are lost in practice.** A 1967 "deposited in the office of this
   Journal" arrangement for *Perception & Psychophysics* predates the Psychonomic Society's
   modern supplementary-materials system by four decades; there is no accessible archive.
   Worth one enquiry to the Psychonomic Society / Springer as a long shot, and to the
   University of Georgia archives for Hodge's papers under contract AF 19(604)-7299, but
   this should not be counted on.
4. Figures 1 and 3 could in principle be digitised, but a scatterplot of (predicted,
   obtained) pairs with no cell labels cannot be reassembled into matrices and in any case
   already presupposes the CRR predictions one wants to recompute.

**However, Hodge (1967) is indispensable as evidence, even though it is useless as data** —
see the quotations below. It documents, in 1967, a *signed, monotone, replicated* residual
in the CRR: accuracy in reduced response sets exceeds the CRR prediction, and the excess
roughly doubles when the set shrinks from 4 to 2. That is a quantitative target a Gaussian
race must reproduce, and the sign and growth pattern are recoverable from Tables 1 and 3
even though the matrices are not.

## What the authors concluded about CRR, quoted verbatim where possible

**Headline conclusion — CRR approximately succeeds:**

> "The results demonstrated: (a) The CRR predicts equally well the response proportions of
> single dimensional visual, kinesthetic, and auditory stimulus ensembles, but less well
> than those for multidimensional auditory stimuli. (b) Better predictions are obtained with
> four than with two stimulus objects. (c) The CRR is sensitive to variations in the spacing
> and range of the stimulus ensembles and to practice on the task. It is concluded that the
> rule tends to fail whenever stimulus conditions elicit differential amounts of stimulus
> and response confusion."

**THE SYSTEMATIC RESIDUAL. This is the passage the present project should quote.** Hodge
sees the residual, measures it, names it "systematic", and then attributes it to a nuisance
factor (response bias) rather than to the wrong choice of noise distribution:

> "The analysis also confirms the apparent concentration of the points below the 45° line,
> the mean of the algebraic differences is +.017. This systematic clustering indicates that
> the CRR slightly underestimated the predicted proportions."

*(The PDF's OCR renders "45°" as "450". A positive entry means obtained > predicted, and
the last column of Tables 1 and 3 is computed on the diagonal cells only — so the claim is
that observed accuracy in the reduced set exceeds the CRR forecast.)*

**The residual grows as the response set shrinks, by a factor of ~2.5:**

> "The less accurate prediction of the 2 by 2 proportions is reflected by the relatively
> large values of the difference measures for these matrices. Of particular interest is the
> finding that the grand mean of the systematic differences is approximately two and one-half
> times larger than the mean of the 4 by 4 matrices (+.044 vs +.017). An examination of the
> individual 2 by 2 matrices showed that the largest systematic differences are associated
> with stimulus pairs consisting of adjacent objects, e.g., stimuli 7, 8; 5,6; 1, 2."

**The residual reverses sign with stimulus spacing** — i.e. it tracks discriminability,
which is exactly what a distributional (d′-based) account predicts and what a
parameter-free odds rule cannot:

> "Except for series C, the mean absolute difference and the percentage of differences
> exceeding .10 in the 4 by 4 data of Table 3 became smaller with an increase in the
> ensemble range. The sign of the algebraic mean shifted from negative to positive,
> indicating that the CRR overestimates the response proportions in the narrowly spaced
> ensembles (Series A and B) and underestimates in the widely spaced ensembles (series C and
> D). Although not as definite, the same trends were present in the 2 by 2 data."

**The residual tracks task performance:**

> "Since the mean proportion correct was always lowest in the second half matrices, the CRR
> predictions are apparently greatly dependent upon task performance; the difference
> measures increase with decreases in the mean proportion correct responses."

**And it shrinks with practice:**

> "All the 4 by 4 and 2 by 2 difference measures in each series declined systematically with
> practice on the 8 by 8 matrix, thus indicating that CRR predictions improve with practice
> on the task."

**Hodge's own interpretation of the signed residual — he reads the sign as a bias/confusion
diagnostic, which is precisely treating it as nuisance:**

> "In evaluating the auditory experiments (Hodge & Pollack, 1962), it was suggested that a
> positive algebraic difference score indicated response confusion and a negative score
> stimulus confusion."

> "A more likely explanation is that single dimensional S-R matrices, in contrast to the
> multidimensional case, are subject to the strong differential effects of stimulus and
> response confusion... In tasks employing multidimensional stimuli, stimulus and response
> confusion are undoubtedly present, but presumably the relevant stimulus variables do not
> engender differential amounts of the two types of confusion. Hence, with stimulus and
> response confusion approximately equal for each stimulus and response, the CRR makes
> excellent predictions."

**The verdict on CRR as a model — useful engineering approximation, unsatisfactory theory:**

> "Taken as a whole, the predictions are good enough to make the CRR a useful tool in
> designing practical stimulus displays and response systems (cf. Engstrand & Moeller,
> 1962). As a model of choice behavior, however, the rule as presently formulated is
> somewhat unsatisfactory. The results of the present experiments and those of Hodge and
> Pollack demonstrate that the CRR has difficulty predicting the response proportions of
> single dimensional ensembles, especially when the 2 by 2 matrices involve variations in
> the spacing-range conditions and practice on the task. While an attempt might be made to
> attribute the difficulties to defects in the experimental procedures, the similarity of
> the results of the various experiments, each of which differed somewhat in procedure,
> argues against such an analysis."

**Final conclusions, verbatim:**

> "In conclusion, the present experiments demonstrate that: (a) CRR predictions are somewhat
> better for multidimensional than for unidimensional stimuli because of the absence of
> large differential amounts of stimulus and response confusion in multidimensional
> situations; (b) the CRR is sensitive to the effects of practice and stimulus spacing-range
> variables; (c) there is little difference in CRR predictions for auditory, visual, and
> kinesthetic stimulus dimensions; and (d) the operation of response biases make testing of
> the CRR with single dimensional stimuli very difficult. More satisfactory tests of the CRR
> will be possible only when response biases can either be controlled experimentally or
> their influence negated theoretically by adding a bias parameter to the model."

**Note for the project:** Hodge's diagnosis — that the residual is caused by "differential
amounts of stimulus and response confusion" and by unequal spacing on an ordered
continuum — is, in modern terms, the statement that the underlying representation is a set
of overlapping distributions of unequal separation. Lee (1968), reviewing exactly this
paper, computed what a Gaussian model implies for the same quantity and reproduced Hodge's
unidimensional/multidimensional dissociation from first principles. See `lee_1968.md`.
