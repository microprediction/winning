# Engstrand & Moeller (1962) — DOES NOT QUALIFY (one-page convention abstract)

## Citation

Engstrand, R. D., & Moeller, G. (1962). The relative legibility of ten simple geometric
figures. *American Psychologist*, 17, 386.

Attested verbatim in the reference list of Hodge (1967), which was fetched and read in full:

> "Engstrand. R. D., & Moeller. G. The relative legibility of ten simple geometric figures.
> Amer. Psychologist, 1962. 17, 386."

**A single page — page 386 of volume 17.** *American Psychologist* volume 17 (1962) carried the
abstracts of papers presented at the APA Annual Convention; a one-page citation in that volume
is a convention abstract of a few hundred words, not a research report.

## Stimuli and master response set

**Ten simple geometric figures**, presumably presented for identification under conditions
manipulating legibility (exposure duration, size, or viewing distance — the standard
display-legibility paradigm of the era). Nothing further can be established from the title and
the one secondary citation.

Master response set: presumably the ten figures. **Unverified.**

## Restricted response sets (nested, overlapping, or a relabelling)

**No evidence of any.** The title is about *relative legibility* — a ranking of ten symbols by
how well each is identified — which is a single-condition, single-response-set design. Nothing
in the title or in Hodge's use of the citation suggests a master-plus-subset structure.

Hodge cites the paper only as an example of an **applied display-design** use of the CRR, not as
a test of it:

> "Taken as a whole, the predictions are good enough to make the CRR a useful tool in designing
> practical stimulus displays and response systems (cf. Engstrand & Moeller, 1962)."

The "cf." and the framing ("designing practical stimulus displays and response systems") place
this as engineering application downstream of the CRR, not as evidence about it. Note also that
Hodge cites it in a sentence about the *utility* of the rule for choosing symbol sets, which is
what a legibility ranking would feed into.

## What numbers are printed (which tables, counts or proportions, per subject or pooled)

**One page total, so at most a legibility ranking or a small summary table.** A 10×10 confusion
matrix would occupy most of a printed page on its own and would be unusual in a convention
abstract. There is no realistic prospect of a master matrix plus restricted-set matrices in a
single page.

## Usability verdict (usable now / needs digitizing / needs library access / unusable, and why)

**UNUSABLE — and not worth pursuing further.**

Three converging reasons:
1. **One page.** The physical extent of the publication rules out a master confusion matrix plus
   one or more restricted-set matrices. Convention abstracts of this era do not carry raw data.
2. **Wrong genre.** A "relative legibility" study ranks symbols; it does not manipulate the
   response set. Nothing in the title or the citing sentence indicates a CRR test.
3. **Wrong role in the literature.** Hodge cites it as an *application* of the CRR to display
   design, not as evidence for or against it. It is not in the list of CRR tests that Hodge and
   Townsend & Landon both enumerate — that list is Anderson 1959, Clarke 1957/1959, Clarke &
   Anderson 1957, Egan 1957, Hodge 1967, Hodge & Pollack 1962, Pollack & Decker 1960,
   Carterette & Wyman 1961. Engstrand & Moeller appears in neither enumeration.

Recommendation: **drop it.** If a completeness check is ever wanted, one PsycEXTRA search behind
an institutional login would settle it in a minute; the expected yield is a 200-word abstract
with a legibility rank ordering and no matrices.

## What the authors concluded about CRR, quoted verbatim where possible

**Nothing — the paper is not about the CRR.** No verbatim quotation is possible; no copy was
obtained.

The only statement connecting this work to the constant-ratio rule is Hodge's, and it is Hodge's
own view rather than Engstrand & Moeller's, and it is a claim about *engineering usefulness*
being compatible with *theoretical inadequacy*:

> "Taken as a whole, the predictions are good enough to make the CRR a useful tool in designing
> practical stimulus displays and response systems (cf. Engstrand & Moeller, 1962). As a model of
> choice behavior, however, the rule as presently formulated is somewhat unsatisfactory."

That sentence pair is the sharpest one-line summary of the whole visual CRR branch: good enough
to build with, wrong as a theory of choice — and nobody in the branch asked whether a Gaussian
race was the missing theory, except Lee (1968), whose calculations went uncited.
