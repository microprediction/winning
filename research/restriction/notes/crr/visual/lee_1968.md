# Lee (1968)

## Citation

Lee, W. (1968). Detection theory, micromatching, and the constant-ratio rule.
*Perception & Psychophysics*, 4(4), 217–219.
doi:10.3758/BF03206305
(University of California, Berkeley; author's note: "Now at Bell Telephone Laboratories,
Holmdel, New Jersey." NIH Grant 11128. Accepted 10 June 1968.)

**Found by forward-citation search on Hodge (1967). This paper is the direct prior art for
the reframing the present project is attempting, and it appears to be entirely forgotten —
it is not cited by Townsend & Landon (1982), by Getty et al. (1979), or by Keren & Baggen
(1981).**

## Stimuli and master response set

**None — this is a theoretical/computational paper with no human subjects and no stimuli.**

The "master set" is a hypothetical set of **three** signals; the analysis is entirely
numerical. Lee assumes:

> "It was assumed that whenever a stimulus is presented to an S it gives rise internally to
> a random independent sample point from a normal (Gaussian) univariate or bivariate
> probability distribution. It was assumed in all cases that every stimulus in each set or
> subset was presented randomly and with equal probability. The standard deviations for all
> distributions in a set or subset were taken to be equal across stimuli and across
> dimensions. For bivariate stimuli, the covariance of the distributions was taken to be
> zero."

Three geometric configurations (Figure 1): **univariate** (three equally spaced normal
densities on a line), **bivariate symmetric** (means at the vertices of an equilateral
triangle), **bivariate orthogonal** (means at the vertices of an isosceles right triangle).
d′ (the distance between means in common-SD units) is swept over 0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0,
2.5, 3.0.

Two response mechanisms are compared against Luce/CRR:
- **Detection theory (DT):** deterministic optimal cutoff points/lines partitioning the
  sample space into response regions, positioned to maximise proportion correct.
  Probabilities obtained "by numerical integration on a computer of the densities for each
  distribution in each response region."
- **Micromatching (MM):** probabilistic mapping, response probability at a sample point
  equals the posterior probability that the point came from that stimulus —
  rⱼ = ∫∫ fⱼ/(f₁+f₂+f₃) dx dy. "Any response has some probability for any sample position."

## Restricted response sets (nested, overlapping, or a relabelling)

**Nested, hypothetical: all subsets of size two drawn from the three-signal master set.**

> "All the cases considered concern sets of three stimuli and subsets of two of these
> stimuli."

For the two-signal subset the optimal DT rule is "a single cutoff point... set down midway
between the means of the two distributions"; in the bivariate cases the two-stimulus
situation reduces to the univariate one "by projecting each sample point onto the line
joining the distribution centers."

Lee defines the quantity of interest — the **"constancy"** — as the ratio of the
restricted-set odds to the master-set odds:

> "Conditional on the presentation of sᵢ when the number of stimuli N = 3, let the response
> probabilities be r₁, r₂, r₃, and let them be r₁′, r₂′ when N = 2. Then, by definition, the
> ratio (rᵢ/rⱼ)/(rᵢ′/rⱼ′) = c_ij. Call c the 'constancy.' According to the CRR, c_ij = 1.0;
> however, as we shall see, there is no necessity that c_ij = 1.0 for detection theory or
> micromatching."

So `constancy = 1` **is** the constant-ratio rule / IIA, and Lee's Table 1 is a table of
*predicted CRR violations under a Gaussian model*.

Constancies are normalised so that c ≤ 1.0 by convention (c_ji = 1/c_ij, so which of the
pair is reported is arbitrary). No empirical data of any kind. The d′ = 0.0 entries are
"not calculations; the values are rational, and in the case of detection theory are limits
as d′ approaches 0.0."

Two closed forms are given in the Discussion: for univariate case (1),
c₁₂ = r₃′/(r₂′ + r₃′) — sic per the OCR, `c₁₂ = r₃/(r₂ + r₃)` in the DT limit — and
analogously for case (3).

## Usability verdict (usable now / needs digitizing / needs library access / unusable, and why)

**Not a data source — but READ IT FIRST. It is a usable *validation target* and the
strongest single piece of evidence that the present project's question is well posed and was
posed before.**

- No confusion matrices, so it cannot enter a forecasting benchmark.
- Table 1 is fully transcribed above and can be **regenerated from scratch** as a unit test:
  any implementation of a Gaussian identification model should reproduce Lee's constancies
  at the stated configurations and d′ values. That is a cheap, exact check on the machinery
  before it is turned on real data.
- **Important caveat for the project.** Lee's two Gaussian mechanisms are *not* the
  Thurstonian race. DT uses deterministic optimal decision boundaries; MM uses posterior
  matching. Neither is "respond with argmax of independent Gaussian draws." The Thurstone
  race is a **third** Gaussian mechanism whose constancies Lee did not compute, and which
  will give different numbers. Lee's own closing remark anticipates this:
  "It is not held that detection theory could not yield any other constancies than the ones
  given... Likewise, various modifications of the idea of a 'stochastic boundary,' such as
  seen in the idea of micromatching, might be investigated, and these would yield different
  constancies. Once such variations are specified, however, the constancies implied can be
  calculated, and these could then be used in diagnosis."
  So computing the Thurstone-race constancy table alongside Lee's DT and MM columns is an
  open, well-defined, and previously invited piece of work.

## What the authors concluded about CRR, quoted verbatim where possible

From the abstract:

> "The conformance of detection theory and micromatching with the constant-ratio rule (CRR)
> was determined by calculations for sets of three stimuli and subsets of two stimuli. For an
> arbitrary selection of sets, the CRR would be expected to hold quite well if either
> detection theory or micromatching lay behind the responses. For specific stimulus
> configurations, however, detection theory and micromatching differ considerably from one
> another and from the CRR. These configurations are found particularly for the univariate
> distributions, and this corresponds to empirical findings that the CRR holds better for
> multidimensional than for unidimensional stimuli. It is shown how the differences between
> the three theories in the implied constancies of ratios can be used in diagnosis of the
> basis of empirical confusion matrices."

**Why CRR looked so good for a decade — a Gaussian model mimics it over most of the
parameter space:**

> "Note that most of the constancies are close to 1.0, and for many situations the CRR would
> hold to a good approximation even if the postulates of detection theory or of micromatching
> lay behind the responses. On the other hand, for some situations, the constancy for
> detection theory, micromatching, or both differs considerably from 1.0, in which case real
> data with some reasonable precision to it must lead to rejection of at least one of the
> three formulations, and possibly of two."

> "Micromatching generally produces constancies closer to 1.0 than detection theory. In fact,
> for an arbitrary selection of configurations, one would expect, on the basis of the
> calculations provided here, that if micromatching lay behind a set of responses, one would
> get a good fit to the CRR."

**How to design a critical test — Lee's prescription, still unexecuted:**

> "It seems clear from the table that if one were selecting cases arbitrarily, the CRR could
> be expected to hold quite well whether detection theory or micromatching were in actuality
> behind the responses. If one wished for a critical test, however, one would not choose
> cases arbitrarily, but would choose cases where the prediction of the CRR, detection
> theory, and micromatching differ considerably. These would be univariate cases, since here
> is where the notable differences occur."

**The Gaussian model explains Hodge's residual — the key claim:**

> "The results in Table I correspond to, and may help explain a generalization noted by Hodge
> and Pollack (1962) and Hodge (1967): the CRR does better for the multidimensional stimuli
> than for the unidimensional stimuli. There is nothing in Luce's choice theory to indicate
> such a result, nor did Clarke indicate any such expectation in his original presentation of
> CRR. Clarke later put forward doubts on the adequacy of the CRR for unidimensional stimuli,
> but these doubts were based on empirical work. The present results perhaps give some notion
> as to why the CRR fares better with multidimensional stimuli: with either detection theory
> or micromatching (or presumably something in between them), constancy is greater for the
> univariate cases than for the bivariate cases."

*(Note: the last clause as printed says "greater for the univariate cases than for the
bivariate cases", which contradicts the abstract and the table — Table 1 shows constancies
nearer 1.0 for the bivariate configurations. This is an error in the published sentence, with
"univariate" and "bivariate" transposed. The abstract, the Discussion's first paragraph, and
Table 1 all agree on the correct direction: departures from CRR are **larger** in the
univariate case.)*

**A worked numerical example of how badly the two Gaussian mechanisms diverge, which doubles
as a demonstration of how much diagnostic power sits in a single 3→2 subset test:**

> "Suppose, e.g., 60% of the responses were correct in the two-choice situation involving s₁
> and s₂ of case (1). Then, assuming detection theory, the value of d′ would be about 0.5.
> The constancy expected would be about .44. On the other hand, assuming micromatching, the
> value of d′ would be about 1.0, and the constancy expected would be about .86. Given that
> 60% of the responses were correct for the set of two stimuli, rather different values for
> the constancy would be predicted assuming detection theory or micromatching."

**A limitation Lee flags, relevant to unidimensional ensembles like Hodge's:**

> "It must be recognized that neither detection theory nor micromatching, as presently
> formulated, can constitute a basis for an adequate psychophysical theory of confusion
> matrices for unidimensional stimuli: One of the more noteworthy findings of recent
> psychophysical research is that the amount of information transmitted in unidimensional
> absolute judgment tasks reaches a maximum with a relatively small number of stimuli—about
> five in the case of loudness or pitch (Garner, 1962, Chap. 3)... Neither detection theory
> nor micromatching allows for such a limitation."

**Individual differences are expected, so pooled data will mislead:**

> "One need not expect that all Ss would have the same pattern of constancies. If the results
> with externally distributed stimuli are any indication (Lee, 1963; Lee & Janke, 1964,
> 1965), some Ss might use rather sharp cutoff boundaries, and others might not."

**Lee's closing plea — a 1968 request for exactly the data this project needs, and an
explanation of why so little of it survives:**

> "In view of the implied effects of the individual stimulus configurations on constancy, I
> would suggest that investigators of the CRR give more detail on individual response
> probabilities for specific configurations, rather than simply giving gross plots and gross
> statistics. If this level of detail is not possible for a journal article, at least the
> availability of the data could be advertized."

Hodge (1967) — published the year before, and cited here — did exactly what Lee complains
about: gross plots (Figures 1, 3) and gross statistics (Tables 1, 3), with the matrices
deposited rather than printed. Getty et al. (1979) and Townsend & Landon (1982) are the two
papers that, apparently without knowing of Lee, did what he asked.
