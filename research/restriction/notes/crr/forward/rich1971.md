# Rich 1971 — Constant ratio rule for confusion matrices from short-term memory experiments

## Citation

Rich, R.F. (1971). Constant ratio rule for confusion matrices from short-term memory experiments. *British Journal of Psychology*, 62(1), 27–35. doi:10.1111/j.2044-8295.1971.tb02007.x. (Department of Psychology, Monash University, Australia.)

The candidate listing flagged this as "OpenAlex W2022525754 — find venue/doi, possibly JASA abstract only". It is **not** a JASA abstract: it is a full 9-page *British Journal of Psychology* article. Venue and DOI resolved.

## Domain and stimuli

Short-term memory for letter sequences (acoustic/articulatory confusion of consonants), the Conrad (1964) paradigm. Rich ran his own STM experiments yielding two **20 × 20** letter confusion matrices, and takes Conrad's (1964) published **10 × 10** letter confusion matrix as the smaller ensemble.

Exact stimulus lists, list lengths, presentation modality, subject counts and the identity of the 20-letter and 10-letter alphabets are not recoverable from the abstract, and I could not obtain the full text (see Access below).

## Master and restricted response sets

Yes, this is a genuine master-plus-restricted CRR design, though **assembled across studies rather than within one experiment**:

- **Master:** two 20 × 20 confusion matrices from Rich's own short-term memory experiments (20-letter ensemble, 20 response alternatives).
- **Restricted:** Conrad's (1964) 10 × 10 confusion matrix (10-letter ensemble). The 10-letter set is a subset of the 20-letter set — the abstract speaks of "the appropriate submatrix of two 20 times 20 matrices", which presupposes containment.
- **Direction of prediction:** exactly the one of interest. The 20 × 20 full-menu matrix is renormalized down onto the surviving 10 letters and the result is compared against Conrad's actually-observed 10 × 10 shares. From the abstract: "The 10 times 10 confusion matrix presented by Conrad (1964) is 'predicted' from the appropriate submatrix of two 20 times 20 matrices, with moderate success."

**Important weakness to record.** The restricted-menu data were collected by a *different experimenter, in a different laboratory, at a different time*, with different subjects. The abstract's own gloss of Clarke's version of the rule — "the subset of a large confusion matrix may be used to predict a smaller confusion matrix based on data gathered under identical conditions" — is precisely the condition Rich's cross-study comparison does **not** satisfy. Any CRR failure is therefore confounded with between-laboratory differences, and any CRR success is partly a statement about how similar two STM labs' confusion structures are. This is materially weaker than Hodge (1967) or Wills et al. (2000), both of which manipulated the menu within one study.

Aggregate-level only (confusion matrices pooled over subjects), as is standard for this literature.

## What numbers are printed or deposited

**Not established.** I could not obtain the full text or a first-page preview, so I cannot confirm that the two 20 × 20 master matrices are printed. Assessment of the prospects:

- **Favourable:** the article runs 9 journal pages (27–35), which is ample for two 20 × 20 tables (roughly two pages), and printing the master matrices is close to mandatory if the reader is to check a renormalization argument. A 1971 *BJP* methods-and-reanalysis paper of this kind would conventionally table them.
- **Also favourable:** the *restricted* side is safe regardless. Conrad, R. (1964), "Acoustic confusions in immediate memory", *British Journal of Psychology* 55, 75–84, is one of the most reproduced matrices in the literature and its 10 × 10 is independently obtainable.
- **Unfavourable:** the master side is the calibration side and is the part that must be printed. If Rich reports only summary discrepancy statistics (the Clarke-style "mean absolute difference" / "% > .10" convention that Hodge 1967 used), the paper collapses to the same failure mode as Hodge.

## Usability verdict

**CRR-TEST-BUT-NUMBERS-NOT-PRINTED** — assigned on the basis that I could not establish that the master matrices are printed, not on positive evidence that they are absent. This is the one candidate in my batch where the verdict could plausibly flip to CRR-TEST-WITH-PRINTED-MATRICES on library access, and it is the single highest-value follow-up in this batch.

If the two 20 × 20 matrices are printed, the paper **would** suffice to score a full-menu-calibrated prediction against CRR on a restricted menu, and at an attractive menu size (20 → 10, far more discriminating between Thurstone and Luce than the 3 → 2 menu in Wills et al. 2000). The restricted-menu target, Conrad (1964) 10 × 10, is independently available, so only Rich's master matrices are needed. Even a single printed 20 × 20 would be enough.

The cross-laboratory confound described above must be stated in any write-up that uses this paper: the full menu and restricted menu were not run under matched conditions, so this is not a clean within-study menu manipulation.

Concrete follow-up: obtain pp. 27–35 of *British Journal of Psychology* 62(1) via interlibrary loan or a subscribing institution and check for printed 20 × 20 tables.

## Conclusion about CRR quoted verbatim

The authors' conclusion, quoted verbatim from the abstract (full text not obtained, so no page number is available for an in-text conclusion; the abstract precedes p. 27):

"Clarke (1957) has presented evidence that a constant ratio rule holds for auditory signal identification experiments. That is, the subset of a large confusion matrix may be used to predict a smaller confusion matrix based on data gathered under identical conditions. Evidence is presented that the constant ratio rule also holds for confusion matrices derived from short-term memory experiments. The 10 times 10 confusion matrix presented by Conrad (1964) is 'predicted' from the appropriate submatrix of two 20 times 20 matrices, with moderate success. Besides confirming the applicability of the constant ratio rule to short-term memory data, the experiment also provides indirect support for Conrad's (1965a) contention that order errors are a consequence of confusions."

Note the hedge in the author's own words: "with moderate success", and the scare quotes around 'predicted'. So Rich is a *pro*-CRR datapoint but a soft one, and no in-text statement of how much deviation was tolerated could be obtained.
