# Hodge & Pollack 1962 — Confusion matrix analysis of single and multidimensional auditory displays

## Citation

Hodge, M. H. and Pollack, I. (1962). "Confusion matrix analysis of single and multidimensional
auditory displays." *Journal of Experimental Psychology* 63(2): 129-142.
doi:10.1037/h0042219. PMID 13907948.
Also issued as Technical Report 60-43, Air Force Command Control Division (Project 7682,
Information Processing by the Human Operator).

## Domain and stimuli

Auditory, but **non-speech**: elementary tonal displays. Nine experiments (eight reported;
Exp. V dropped for insufficient data), one crew of 7 university students (6 responding at a
time), all with prior psychophysical experience, run 8 h/day for 3 months after 10 days of
practice to asymptote.

Stimulus ensembles, always of size eight at the top level:

- **Single-dimensional.** Exp. I: eight durations of a 1000-cps tone, .185-.651 s, log-spaced,
  80 dB. Exp. IV: eight intensities, 3 dB apart, lowest 70 dB SPL, 1000 cps, .32 s.
  Exp. III and IX: 21 four-element ensembles of durations (III) / intensities (IX) spanning
  16 steps, used to vary ensemble *range* around a fixed common stimulus pair.
  Exp. VII: four series A/B/C/D of eight intensities spaced 4, 2, 1 and 0.5 dB apart.
- **Multidimensional.** Exp. II ("coarse"): eight objects from the 2x2x2 combination of
  frequency (1000/1025 cps), duration (.255/.367 s) and level (80/84 dB). Exp. VIII ("fine"):
  same design with 1000/1006 cps, .320/.324 s, 80/81 dB. Exp. VI ("redundant"): eight objects
  from 2 frequencies x 4 durations x 4 intensities with duration and intensity made redundant.

Responses were push buttons labelled 1-8 (single-dimensional) or labelled on all three
dimensions, e.g. "low, short, soft" (multidimensional). Correct answer fed back each trial;
monetary incentive on per-subject correct count. Responses of the six listeners summed
directly into counters so the complete stimulus-response matrix was available in real time.

## Master and restricted response sets

Yes — and the paper is explicit that both stimuli *and* their associated responses are removed,
which is exactly the restricted-menu operation. From the definitions (p. 130):

> "We first obtain the initial 4 x 4 matrix as shown in Table 1. We then dispose of Stimuli III
> and IV and of Responses 3 and 4. Finally, the remaining 2 X 2 matrix is normalized so that
> its conditional response probabilities sum to 1.0. The rule predicts that the resulting
> derived confusability will be identical with the observed confusability of a matrix consisting
> of Stimuli I and I] and Responses 1 and 2."

- **Master menu:** the **8 x 8** matrix over the full eight-object ensemble, in each of Exps.
  I, IV, VII (four series), II, VIII, VI.
- **Restricted menus:** separately run **4 x 4** and **2 x 2** identification tests over
  subsets of the same eight objects, with the button set and labels cut down accordingly. In
  Exp. VII the paper notes "Within a given series the labels employed in the 8 X 8, 4 X 4, and
  2 X 2 tests were identical", so response labelling is held fixed across menu size. Exp. VI
  ran only the 8x8 and ten 4x4 matrices. Table 3 (p. 133) gives, per experiment and per matrix
  size, the number of observations of each stimulus and (in parentheses) the number of matrices
  of that size.
- Order was partially counterbalanced: half the 8x8 responses at the start and half at the end
  of Exp. I, 4x4 data before 2x2 data; Exp. VII used a two-block randomisation of matrix size.

The CRR is scored exactly as the project does it — full-menu shares renormalised onto the
surviving subset, then compared cell-by-cell to the separately observed restricted-menu shares.

## What numbers are printed or deposited

**No confusion matrices are printed at all — neither master nor restricted.** Everything is
reported as summary discrepancy statistics or as predicted-vs-obtained scatterplots. Specifically:

- **Table 1 (p. 130)** is a purely *algebraic/schematic* 4x4 matrix with letter entries a, b,
  ..., p, used to define derived confusability. No data.
- **Table 2 (p. 132)** is a schematic layout of the 21 stimulus ensembles of Exps. III and IX
  (open circles marking which of 16 steps were used), with two right-hand columns giving the
  *derived confusability score* for the common stimulus pair. Observed confusability for the
  common pairs is given only in running text as three numbers per experiment (14, 25, 24% for
  Exp. III; 39, 35, 32% for Exp. IX).
- **Table 3 (p. 133)** gives observation counts and matrix counts only.
- **Table 4 (p. 134)** is a schematic of the four interstimulus spacings in Exp. VII.
- **Table 5 (p. 134)** gives observed (in parentheses) and derived confusability *scores* for
  selected stimulus pairs in Exp. VII — pair-level summaries, not matrices.
- **Table 6 (p. 137)** and **Table 7 (p. 138)** are the main results: per experiment and per
  matrix size (8x8 / 4x4 / 2x2, each split into first and second half by rank of proportion
  correct), three columns — mean proportion correct, mean absolute predicted-obtained
  difference, and mean signed difference of the diagonal entries. E.g. Exp. I: 4x4 mean
  |diff| = .028, mean diagonal diff = +.014; 2x2 mean |diff| = .082, mean diagonal diff =
  +.052. Exp. IV: .025 / +.013 for 4x4, .043 / +.042 for 2x2. Exp. VII series A-D diagonal
  differences +.053, +.006, -.068, -.006.
- **Figures 1, 2, 4, 5, 6** are scatterplots of obtained proportion against predicted
  proportion, one point per 4x4 and per 2x2 cell, for Exps. I, IV, II, VIII, VI respectively.
  These are the only cell-level records and they are unlabelled point clouds — digitising them
  would give (predicted, obtained) pairs but with no way to attribute a point to a stimulus,
  a response, or even to a particular subset matrix.
- **Figure 3 (p. 139)** plots confusability against interstimulus distance for Exp. I.
- No data deposit (1962). The underlying counters' output is not archived anywhere I could find.

Also reported in text: percentage of cells whose predicted-obtained difference exceeds .10 —
Exp. I 4.2% (4x4) and 28.6% (2x2); Exp. IV 2.8% and 21.5%; multidimensional Exps. II and VIII
under 2% for both 4x4 and 2x2.

## Usability verdict

**Not sufficient.** The design is precisely what the project wants — an 8-alternative master
menu and separately-run 4- and 2-alternative restricted menus over the same stimulus objects,
with responses removed along with stimuli, replicated across six ensembles (two
single-dimensional, one spacing series of four, three multidimensional) — but **no confusion
matrix is printed anywhere in the paper**, so there is nothing to calibrate a Thurstone model
on and nothing to score against. The scatterplots (Figs. 1, 2, 4, 5, 6) hold cell-level
(predicted, obtained) pairs but with no stimulus/response labels, so they cannot be reassembled
into matrices; at best they support a distributional comparison. The printed mean |diff| and
mean signed diagonal difference in Tables 6 and 7 are usable as a *benchmark to beat* only if
the raw matrices are recovered from elsewhere, which they cannot be.

There is one genuinely valuable qualitative finding for the project, independent of the
numbers: CRR fails **systematically and in a signed, structured way** — it over-predicts
accuracy for 2x2 menus drawn from *adjacent* objects in *widely spaced* ensembles, and the
authors attribute this to confusion among *response labels* rather than among stimuli
(pp. 138-139). That is a context effect on the response set, i.e. exactly an IIA violation
driven by menu composition.

Classification: **CRR-TEST-BUT-NUMBERS-NOT-PRINTED**.

## Conclusion about CRR quoted verbatim

From the Summary, p. 142:

> "The results indicate: (a) In general, the CRR makes excellent predictions of the response
> probabilities of the single and multidimensional matrices. The notable exceptions occur when
> predictions for 2 X 2 matrices are made from adjacent objects in widely spaced ensembles.
> (b) The CRR predicts the probabilities of the multidimensional matrices somewhat better than
> those of the single-dimensional ensembles"

From the Discussion, p. 139:

> "The serious failures of the CRR are associated with adjacent steps where the stimulus (and
> response) spacing is wide. In these instances the predicted proportions are seriously
> underestimated relative to the obtained proportions."

And on the disagreement with Clarke's own negative result for tones, p. 141:

> "The successful application of the rule in the single-dimensional cases of the present
> experiments is directly opposite Clarke's (1959) finding that responses to intensity and
> frequency stimuli cannot be described by the CRR. Clarke's difficulty with the rule, however,
> was due to strong contextual effects present in his data."

(The last sentence is footnoted to "F. R. Clarke, personal communication, 1960" — i.e. Clarke's
1959 JASA abstract reported a CRR *failure* for tonal displays, and its data were never
published.)
