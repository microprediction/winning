## Citation

Wills, A. J., Reimers, S., Stewart, N., Suret, M., & McLaren, I. P. L. (2000). Tests of
the ratio rule in categorization. *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology A*, 53(4),
983-1011. DOI 10.1080/02724980050156263.

## Domain and stimuli

Two categorization experiments with artificial visual prototype-structured stimuli built
from 12 symbols positioned on a grid.

## Master response set and restricted response sets (nested, overlapping, or a relabelling)

**No restriction — two categories throughout.** The ratio rule is tested here in its
other guise: whether psychological magnitudes map to choice probabilities via proportional
normalization, given an independent handle on the magnitudes (the count of
category-appropriate symbols). There is no master set and no restricted set, so it is not a
CRR test in our sense.

## What numbers are printed or deposited (which tables/files, counts or proportions, per subject or pooled)

Not checked at cell level. With two categories, per-stimulus response proportions would
suffice, and the paper does model individual choice proportions, so something may be
tabulated.

## Access (a DIRECT url you fetched; open, paywalled, or Wayback-only)

**Paywalled** at SAGE; a full text is listed on ResearchGate and in the Plymouth
institutional repository. I did not fetch any copy.

## Usability verdict (usable now / needs digitizing / needs library access / unusable, and why)

**Unusable** for the restricted-response-set test — no restriction. Recorded because it
is the closest prior art to the project's *conclusion* rather than its method, and because
missing it and then rediscovering its result would be embarrassing.

## What the authors concluded, quoted verbatim where possible

From the abstract, which states the project's thesis twenty-six years early:

> "The ratio rule was shown to be incorrect for these experiments, given the assumption that
> the magnitude terms for each category are univariate functions of the number of
> category-appropriate symbols contained in the presented stimulus. A connectionist
> winner-take-all model of categorical decision was shown to account for the data, with the
> central feature underlying the model's success being the assumption that categorical
> decisions are based on a Thurstonian choice process whose noise distribution is not double
> exponential in form."

Quoted from the published abstract as returned by search, not from the paper itself.

## Full text obtained 2026-08-19, and it changes the reading

Not paywalled after all. The author's own publications page links a full-text PDF on his site:

    https://www.andywills.info/assets/pdf/2000Wills.pdf     375,808 bytes, 29 pages

Unpaywall reports the DOI as closed and the earlier search of ResearchGate and the Plymouth
repository found nothing, so the lesson is to check the author's homepage before recording a
source as blocked. The title page confirms five authors including Suret, whom the lab's own
publication list omits.

THREE CLAIMS PREVIOUSLY TAKEN FROM THE ABSTRACT, ALL VERIFIED VERBATIM:

- "the noise added to nu_i ranges from +N to -N, has a mean of zero, and has a rectangular
  distribution" (p. 830 of the extracted text), so the positive model is not Gaussian.
- "The WTA model is a relatively complex system with four free parameters (E, D, N, and S). The
  ratio rule, in contrast, has no free parameters."
- "S is set to 0.18 for the two-choice condition, 0.65 for the three-choice condition", so a
  parameter is set per menu and the restricted account is not a prediction from full-menu
  quantities.

AND ONE FINDING THAT CUTS THE OTHER WAY, WHICH THE ABSTRACT DID NOT REVEAL. They also build a
parameter-free race and say so:

> "The most basic instantiation of the central 'pick the biggest' principle underlying the WTA
> model would be to take the instantaneous value of each of the noisy magnitude terms and pick
> the alternative with the largest value at that instant. ... Like the ratio rule, the simple-WTA
> system has no free parameters"

and having run it with rectangular noise they add:

> "Employing Gaussian distributions with a standard deviation of 0.28 (which have a rectangular
> equivalent with a width of 0.7) produces comparable results."

So a parameter-free contest, including a Gaussian one, was constructed and compared against the
ratio rule in 2000. They set it aside because its correspondence with the data "is by no means as
good" as the fitted four-parameter model's. What they judge by is the shape of derived functions,
not held-out score, and they never compute numerically the parameter-free point prediction their
own Equations 3 and 6 imply for the restricted share. That is what remains ours, and the paper's
prior-art paragraph now says all of this rather than the thinner version taken from the abstract.

The PDF is not committed here, since it is a publisher-typeset copy hosted by the author; the URL
and the verified quotations are the record.
