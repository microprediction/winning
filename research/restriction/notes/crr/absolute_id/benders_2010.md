## Citation

Benders, T., & Escudero, P. (2010). The interrelation between the stimulus range and the
number of response categories in vowel categorization. *Interspeech 2010*, 106-109.
DOI 10.21437/Interspeech.2010-61. Amsterdam Center for Language and Communication.

## Domain and stimuli

Speech: vowel categorization along a 91-step F1 continuum between Peruvian Spanish /i/
and /e/, with duration co-varied. Three stimulus range conditions (low, intermediate, high),
counterbalanced within participant.

## Master response set and restricted response sets (nested, overlapping, or a relabelling)

**Nested response labels, but between subjects.** Half the participants had two response
categories (/i/, /e/); the other half had five, the extra ones being other Spanish vowels. So
the two-category label set is a subset of the five-category label set over identical stimuli
— genuinely nested at the label level, and the identity of /i/ and /e/ does not change.

Two limits. The manipulation is between participants, so no within-subject contrast. And with
2 versus 5 categories on a 1-D continuum between two of them, this is closer to a boundary-shift
paradigm than a confusion-matrix paradigm.

## What numbers are printed or deposited (which tables/files, counts or proportions, per subject or pooled)

**Nothing usable at cell level.** Table 1 reports beta-coefficient estimates, standard
deviations and significance from a hierarchical logistic regression. Figure 2 plots boundary
locations for the five-category (panel a) and two-category (panel b) groups. Per-stimulus
response proportions — which with 2-5 categories would *be* the confusion matrix — are not
tabulated. No data availability statement; Interspeech papers rarely have one.

## Usability verdict (usable now / needs digitizing / needs library access / unusable, and why)

**Unusable** — manipulates the number of response categories with nested labels, but
publishes only regression coefficients and boundary locations, no cell-level shares.

Recorded because the *design* is a cheap template outside psychophysics proper, and because
the intro points at a related study (their reference 15) where "in a first condition, only
tokens from the continuum from /i/ to /e/ were presented, while in a second condition, the
same stimuli were presented interspersed" with a third vowel, half the listeners having three
response categories and half two. That cited study may print more, and is worth chasing if
the speech branch needs another source.

## What the authors concluded, quoted verbatim where possible

On the mechanism they propose, which is the same context-dependence Getty reports:

> "boundary shifts result from participants' strategy to evenly distribute their responses
> over the available categories"

> "We hypothesize that the number of response categories available to listeners can change
> their perception of stimuli"

And the observed interaction: "the boundary shifts for the twocategory group (panel b) are
larger than those for the fivecategory group (panel a) in both the low and the high ranges."
