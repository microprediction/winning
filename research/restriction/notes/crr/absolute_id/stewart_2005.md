## Citation

Stewart, N., Brown, G. D. A., & Chater, N. (2005). Absolute identification by relative
judgment. *Psychological Review*, 112(4), 881-911. DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.112.4.881.

## Domain and stimuli

Experiment 1: 120 Warwick undergraduates, tones varying in frequency, seven blocks of
120 tones. Two crossed between-subject factors — spacing (narrow, 6% steps, total range
530 Hz; wide, 12% steps, total range 1063.85 Hz) and set size (6, 8, 10). Experiment 2:
19 participants, 10 tones at 25% spacing, feedback manipulation.

## Master response set and restricted response sets (nested, overlapping, or a relabelling)

**Nested stimuli, but a relabelling of responses. Verified from the text.** The stimulus
nesting is genuine and deliberate: "In the set-size-8 conditions, only the middle 8 tones
were used. Similarly, in the set-size-6 conditions, only the middle 6 tones were used", with
the wide and narrow ranges sharing a geometric mean.

But the response labels are within-set ranks: "For half of the participants in each cell,
tones were numbered from lowest to highest, and for the other half of the participants, this
mapping was reversed. Participants were told that each tone was 1 of a set of 6, 8, or 10
varying in frequency and were also told the ordering of the responses."

So label 1 denotes a different physical tone in each condition. Because the mapping is
order-preserving over physically identical nested tone sets, it is in principle invertible —
set-size-8 label k corresponds to set-size-10 label k+1 — so this *would* be recoverable as
a nested response set if the data existed. It does not (below), so the point is moot.

Also between-subjects, so no within-subject contrast.

## What numbers are printed or deposited (which tables/files, counts or proportions, per subject or pooled)

**Nothing at cell level, and the raw data is lost.** The paper reports information
transmitted, accuracy and sequential-effect statistics.

The trial data was once posted. The archived directory listing at
`http://web.archive.org/web/20070819143044/http://stewart.psych.warwick.ac.uk/publications/RJM_reply/`
(fetched, HTTP 200) names:

    Brown_Neath_Chater_2002_best_fits.csv    11K   13-Nov-2006
    Kent_Lamberts_2005_best_fits.csv        650    13-Nov-2006
    Lacouture_1997_best_fits.csv            6.2K   13-Nov-2006
    Stewart_Brown_Chater_2005_best_fits.csv  15K   13-Nov-2006
    Stewart_Brown_Chater_2005_data.zip      380K   13-Nov-2006
    source_code/

**None of the data files were ever captured.** Fetching the zip from Wayback returns 404, and
a CDX sweep of the whole host shows captures only of the HTML directory listings and of
`source_code/` (`makefile`, `modified_RJM.c`). The zip is gone.

Warwick's institutional dataset record confirms it independently. WRAP eprint 135127, "Why
unidimensional identification is so poor: modelling a core cognitive limit", states: "Data
record is empty. The Data Collection only consists of metadata and documentation as the data
could not be archived due to legal, ethical or commercial constraints."

Neil Stewart's current public repositories contain no absolute-identification data: 22 GitHub
repos (all decision-making, risky choice, loss aversion) and 10 OSF projects (same), checked
via the GitHub and OSF APIs.

## Usability verdict (usable now / needs digitizing / needs library access / unusable, and why)

**Unusable.** Set size manipulated, nested stimuli, but responses relabelled to 1..N,
between subjects, only aggregate statistics printed, and the raw data was deleted from the
web without ever being archived. Do not search again — the loss is documented above. The
only remaining route is to email Stewart, Brown or Chater directly.

## What the authors concluded, quoted verbatim where possible

On the set-size effect they were modelling:

> "In unidimensional absolute identification tasks, participants identify stimuli that vary
> along a single dimension. Performance is surprisingly poor compared with discrimination of
> the same stimuli."

They report a small asymmetry that they corrected out, worth knowing if anyone recovers the
data: "There was a significant, t(118) = 5.07, p < .0001, but small (3%) accuracy advantage
for higher pitched stimuli. This suggests that stimuli were not quite psychologically evenly
spaced but that higher pitched stimuli were more widely spaced."
