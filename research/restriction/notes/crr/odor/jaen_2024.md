# Jaén et al. 2024 — NIH Toolbox Odor ID via Brain Health Registry, descriptors as words

## Citation
Jaén C, Maute C, Mackin S, Camacho MR, Truran D, Nosheny R, Weiner MW, Dalton P. "Remote olfactory
assessment using the NIH Toolbox Odor Identification test and the brain health registry."
*PLoS ONE* 2024;19(4):e0301264. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0301264. PMC11025917.
(Verified via Europe PMC core record.)

## Domain and stimuli
Olfaction, cued identification. NIH Toolbox Odor Identification Test, 9 odorants, 4-AFC with both
picture and English word labels. Two samples: an in-person Monell cohort (n=1,163) and a remote Brain
Health Registry cohort (n=845).

## Master response set and restricted response sets (nested, overlapping, or a relabelling)
**No restriction — fixed 4-alternative list per odor.** But the overlap structure is unusually tight
and worth recording.

All nine items draw their 4-option menus from a universe of only **10 descriptors**:
{Bubble Gum, Chocolate, Cinnamon, Coffee, Flower, Lemon, Natural Gas, Play Doh, Popcorn, Smoke}.
Flower distracts on four items and is the target on a fifth; Chocolate is target once and distractor
on four; Cinnamon appears *only* as a distractor and is never a target. So this is choice from
heavily overlapping 4-subsets of a small shared alternative universe — the closest thing in open
olfactory data to varying menus over a common set.

The confound is unavoidable and must be stated: the odor changes with the menu, so you cannot compare
across items as if the stimulus were held fixed. Within an item the menu is constant. It is therefore
overlapping-but-not-nested, and not a regularity test.

## What numbers are printed or deposited (which tables/files, counts or proportions, per subject or pooled)
**Per-subject, and the chosen descriptor is stored as a literal word — no code table needed.**

Supporting file S1 (`pone.0301264.s001.xlsx`, 891,537 bytes), three sheets:
- Sheet 1 "Monell Data": **1,163 rows × 9 odor columns, each cell the chosen descriptor's name**, plus
  MONELL id, Gender, Age, Race, Ethnicity. This is the usable sheet.
- Sheet 2 "NIH Toolbox ODOR ID scores": total scores only.
- Sheet 3 "NIH Toolbox Sensory items": the 845 remote respondents, Yes/No per item only.

I downloaded and parsed sheet 1. Needs light cleaning — trailing-space and case variants (`"Lemon "`,
`"bubble gum"`, `"Chocolate "`). After normalizing, my shares (n=1,163 per item):

| Target | Correct | Distractor shares |
|---|---|---|
| Lemon | 0.813 | cinnamon .095, coffee .054, popcorn .038 |
| Play Doh | 0.720 | chocolate .150, flower .068, cinnamon .061 (+1 stray coffee) |
| Bubble Gum | 0.856 | chocolate .095, coffee .035, popcorn .014 |
| Chocolate | 0.702 | **flower .252**, bubble gum .032, lemon .015 |
| Popcorn | 0.859 | flower .060, play doh .050, chocolate .031 |
| Coffee | 0.915 | natural gas .053, popcorn .018, lemon .014 |
| Smoke | 0.813 | flower .114, play doh .064, chocolate .008 |
| Natural Gas | 0.959 | coffee .015, lemon .015, bubble gum .011 |
| Flower | 0.941 | cinnamon .028, smoke .025, popcorn .006 |

The Chocolate→Flower rate of 25% is the standout systematic confusion.

## Usability verdict (usable now / needs digitizing / needs library access / unusable, and why)
**Usable now.** Second-best zero-friction source after `nhanes_2014.md`, and easier to read because
the responses are already words rather than codes — no answer-key reconstruction, no risk of
mis-mapping. 1,163 subjects × 9 items = ~10,500 choices with demographics attached.

Caveat: only 9 items, and the same fixed-menu limitation as NHANES — it anchors choice shares but
cannot by itself test a menu effect. Note this is the *exception* among NIH Toolbox publications;
see `dalton_2013.md` for why the instrument's own literature is otherwise percent-correct only.

## What the authors concluded, quoted verbatim where possible
The paper's purpose is methodological — whether olfactory testing can be done remotely and
self-administered at registry scale. The finding is that remote NIH Toolbox odor identification is
feasible and yields data comparable to in-person administration, supporting its use in large
cognitive-aging registries. The per-descriptor detail in S1 is incidental to that argument and is not
analyzed in the paper at all, which is why it survived into the supplement in raw form.
