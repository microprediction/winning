# Lötsch, Hummel & Ultsch — large Dresden Sniffin' Sticks analyses, no data deposited

## Citation
Lötsch J, Hummel T, Ultsch A. Machine-learning analyses of Sniffin' Sticks data, *Scientific Reports*
2023 (n = 10,714). Related: "Machine-learned analysis of side-differences in odor identification
performance" (2019); "Sorting of Odor Dilutions Is a Meaningful Addition to Assessments of Olfactory
Function..." (2022, PMID 35887775). Exact article titles/volumes for the 2023 paper were not
independently reconfirmed.

## Domain and stimuli
Olfaction. Very large clinical Sniffin' Sticks datasets from the TU Dresden Smell & Taste Clinic —
identification, discrimination and threshold subtests, 4-AFC identification.

## Master response set and restricted response sets (nested, overlapping, or a relabelling)
**Fixed 4 alternatives.** No menu manipulation in any of these papers.

## What numbers are printed or deposited (which tables/files, counts or proportions, per subject or pooled)
**Nothing deposited.** Results appear only in the articles' own tables and figures; the machine-learning
code is described but the clinical data is not released. With n = 10,714 this is the largest Sniffin'
Sticks corpus referenced anywhere in this directory, and none of it is public.

A separate Dresden dataset of 1,422 results "assembled over the last 8 years at science fairs" using the
16-item identification subtest is referenced in the literature (surfaced via a HAL record) but no
deposit was located for it either.

## Usability verdict (usable now / needs digitizing / needs library access / unusable, and why)
**Unusable as published.** Nothing per-descriptor is printed, so there is nothing to digitize.

Relevant chiefly as context for the `negoias_2010.md` data request: Hummel's group holds enormous
quantities of item-level Sniffin' Sticks data and has a consistent practice of **not depositing it**. That
lowers the prior on a successful data request but raises the payoff if one succeeds — and the same email
could reasonably ask about both the 2010 crossover sheets and the larger clinical corpus.

## What the authors concluded, quoted verbatim where possible
Not verified verbatim. The general thrust of the machine-learning papers is that combining olfactory
subtests (and adding new ones, such as dilution sorting) improves classification of olfactory dysfunction
beyond any single subtest score.
