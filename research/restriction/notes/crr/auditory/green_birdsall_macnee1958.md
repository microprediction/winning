# Green, Birdsall & Macnee (1958) — vocabulary size and articulation score, from the signal-detection side. GREEN OPEN ACCESS.

## Citation

Green, D. M., Birdsall, T. G., & Macnee, A. B. (1958). *The effect of vocabulary size on articulation
score.* University of Michigan (Electronic Defense Group / Cooley Electronics Laboratory technical
report series). Handle: hdl.handle.net/2027.42/5213

Recovered from the reference list of Holloway (1970), fetched from OpenAlex. **Green and Birdsall are two
of the four authors of the foundational signal-detection-theory papers** (Peterson, Birdsall & Fox 1954;
Swets, Tanner & Birdsall 1961; Green & Swets 1966). This makes the item unusually important for the
project's framing: it is the Gaussian/detection-theory camp addressing response-set size in speech, in
1958, one year before Clarke (1959) put the CRR and "the theory of signal detectability" head to head.

## Stimuli and master response set

Speech material; **vocabulary size is the independent variable** and articulation score (percent correct
word identification) is the dependent variable. Exact vocabularies and sizes: not recoverable without the
document, which I could not open (see Access).

The Michigan Electronic Defense Group was the home of the detection-theory group; its technical report
series is where the SDT treatment of the "n alternatives" problem was worked out. The companion result in
the SDT literature is that percent correct in an m-alternative forced choice among equally discriminable
signals is a fixed function of d' and m — which is exactly the "simplified version of the theory of
signal detectability" that Clarke (1959) tested against "the simplified version of the constant-ratio
rule".

## Restricted response sets (state whether nested, overlapping, or a relabelling)

**Nested, and vocabulary size is the manipulation** — that is the entire subject of the report. Whether
the smaller vocabularies are literal subsets of the larger ones, or independently drawn word lists of
different size, is not verified. In the intelligibility-testing tradition of the period (PB word lists,
rhyme tests) both practices existed, and it matters: only literal subsets support a CRR test.

If both answers are favourable this is a significant find: a 1958 master-plus-restriction speech dataset
from the signal-detection group, predating Clarke's own CRR-versus-SDT comparison, and free. If not, it
still earns a citation, because it establishes that the detection-theory camp was working on
response-set-size effects in speech at the same moment and in the same idiom as the CRR camp — and that
the two literatures were measuring the same scalar (percent correct) that Clarke (1959) then showed could
not tell them apart.

Related, and in the same category of "manipulates set size, reports only percent correct": Pollack's
*Message Uncertainty and Message Reception* series, whose abstracts I fetched from Crossref (all HTTP 200,
open; the papers themselves paywalled):

- Pollack, I. (1959), *JASA* **31**, 1500-1508, doi:10.1121/1.1907655. Verbatim: "*Accuracy of message
  reception was found to be independent of the message-source uncertainty, but critically dependent upon
  the response uncertainty - or more exactly, upon the size of the set of relevant response categories.*"
- Pollack, I. (1960), "Message Uncertainty and Message Reception. II", *Language and Speech* **3**,
  174-177, doi:10.1177/002383096000300306. Verbatim: "*A previous experiment indicated (1) that the
  accuracy of message reception is relatively independent of the size of the message ensemble if the number
  of response alternatives is held constant; and (2) that the accuracy of monitoring performance is
  independent of the number of irrelevant response alternatives. The present study attempts to determine
  whether the same generalizations are warranted in the case where the message and response alternatives
  are randomly chosen from the English language. They are.*"
- Pollack, I. (1963), "Message-uncertainty and message-reception: III. Effect of restriction of verbal
  context", *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*, doi:10.1016/s0022-5371(63)80024-9.

**All three are one-line unusable for this project: they manipulate response-set size and publish only
accuracy of message reception, not confusion matrices.** They are, however, the sharpest statement in the
literature that it is the *response* set and not the *stimulus* set that governs performance — which is
the empirical premise the whole CRR programme rests on. Worth one sentence and a citation.

## What the authors concluded about CRR, quoted verbatim where possible

**No text from this report was obtained; no verbatim quotation from Green, Birdsall & Macnee is
available.** The report predates the naming of the constant-ratio rule (Clarke 1957 named it in 1957 but
the report is from the detection-theory tradition), and there is no reason to expect it to mention the CRR
at all.

The nearest verbatim statement of the CRR-versus-detection-theory question from the same year and the same
funding programme is Clarke (1959), *JASA* **31**, 835 (a one-page meeting abstract, whose full text I
obtained from Crossref, HTTP 200):

> "The effect of the number of stimulus-response alternatives on the human observer's probability of making
> a correct response was predicted by three models: a simple threshold model; the constant-ratio rule with
> simplifying assumptions; and the theory of signal detectability with simplifying assumptions. ... The
> simple threshold model failed to account for any of the data. The simplified version of the constant-ratio
> rule and the simplified version of the theory of signal detectability were both compatible with the data
> obtained in the speech experiments."

See `clarke1959.md`. That "both compatible" is the crux: on percent correct, Gaussian and Gumbel could not
be separated in 1959, and nobody went back to the matrices to separate them.
