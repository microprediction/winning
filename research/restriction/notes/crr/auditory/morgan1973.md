# Morgan, Chambers & Morton (1973) — four complete 9x9 digit matrices, OBTAINED; but NO restricted response sets

## Citation

Morgan, B. J. T., Chambers, S. M., & Morton, J. (1973). Acoustic confusion of digits in memory and
recognition. *Perception & Psychophysics*, **14**(2), 375-383. doi:10.3758/BF03212408
M.R.C. Applied Psychology Unit, 15 Chaucer Road, Cambridge, CB2 2EF, England.
(Author notes: Morgan "*Now at the Mathematical Institute, University of Kent, Canterbury, Kent,
England*"; Chambers "*Now at the Department of Psychology, Monash University, Clayton, Victoria 3168,
Australia*".) Received 11 December 1972; revision received 30 April 1973.

**PDF obtained and fully text-extracted. This is the only member of the auditory branch that is both
free and complete.**

## Stimuli and master response set

Spoken **digits 1-9** — a nine-item master set, four times over. Two tasks, two voices each:

**(a) Recognition in noise.** Digits masked by white noise at approximately -10 dB S/N, chosen by
pretest so that listeners "*would be making the target of 50% errors*"; final overall error rate 5.3%
(as OCR'd — "5 3~"). Blocks of 135 items (15 presentations of each digit), balanced for serial
position. Listeners wrote the digit, guessing if unsure rather than leaving blanks. 39 subjects heard
one female-voice block; a further 28 heard four blocks (two female, two male). Voices: S.C. (female,
Australian) and J.M. (male, English).

**(b) Serial recall (memory).** Pooled from 29 separate experiments in which subjects heard lists of 7
or 8 digits at 2/sec and recalled them serially. J.F. (female, American): 230 subjects, 23,652 lists,
12 experiments. S.C. (female, Australian): 328 subjects, 24,750 lists, 17 experiments. Only lists with
exactly one error were included ("*This restriction was necessary as we were interested only in errors
brought about by confusions between digits*"). Overall error proportion 6.5%. Subjects were "*housewives,
aged 20 to 60, drawn from the APU Ss panel*".

## Restricted response sets (state whether nested, overlapping, or a relabelling)

**NONE. There is no restricted response set anywhere in this paper.** All four matrices are 9x9 over
the identical master set {1,...,9}.

The four matrices vary the *task* (recognition vs recall) and the *talker* (JM, SC, JF), not the
response set. So on the brief's inclusion criterion — a master matrix plus one or more restricted
response sets over the same stimuli — **this paper does not qualify.** It is filed here because:

1. It is the only free, complete, verified master matrix in the branch, so it is the natural substrate
   for any restriction study that has to be run afresh.
2. It carries the Cambridge group's explicit rejection of the CRR (quoted below).
3. Its first author is the author of Morgan (1974), the paper that broke the CRR corpus.

Adjacent paper worth noting, by the same author, also an auditory master with no restrictions:
Morgan (1973a), "A letter-digit matrix of auditory confusions", *British Journal of Psychology*,
doi:10.1111/j.2044-8295.1973.tb01384.x — **35 elements** (all letters and digits). Referenced in this
paper (p. 380): "*a MDSCAL analysis of the acoustic confusions in a letter-digit alphabet containing all
the letters and digits (35 elements in all) has resulted in a fairly good fit in three dimensions
(stress = 12.50%)*". A 35x35 auditory master would be an excellent base for constructing restricted
sets, and it is the matrix Morgan (1974) had in hand when he wrote about predicting "small vocabularies"
from a "full alphabetic confusion matrix".

| Table | Test statistic |

Verbatim on interpretation: "*As x^2_55 (0.1%) = 90, the absurdly high values above imply rejection of
H0 in all four cases. This was a case when the data were so obviously significant that a statistical
test was really unnecessary!*"

The null hypothesis tested is **exactly the CRR/Luce form**, verbatim: "*The test developed by Morgan
tests the null hypothesis, H0: p_ij = p_j/(1 - p_i), i != j, where p_ij is the probability that Response
j is made for Stimulus i, and the (p_i) form a probability distribution*", and "*it can be shown that
(see Altham, 1970) H0 is equivalent to the following hypothesis (for more than three stimuli):
p_ik/p_il = p_jk/p_jl for all possible sets of four (i, j, k, l) so that no two are the same. This is
clearly what we mean when we talk in terms of equal confusability.*"

That cross-ratio condition is proportional renormalization. **So Tables 1-4 with Table 5 constitute a
published, four-fold, overwhelming rejection of the constant-ratio form on auditory digit confusions —
in 1973, with the matrices printed.**

## Usability verdict (usable now / needs digitizing / needs library access / unusable, and why)

**USABLE NOW — already digitized above, and fully arithmetic-checked.** Four 9x9 count matrices with
N = 4,809 / 14,006 / 23,652 / 24,750 responses respectively. No further acquisition needed.

But be clear about what it can and cannot do for the project:

- **CANNOT** serve as a master-plus-restriction test on its own. There are no restricted response sets.
- **CAN** serve as (i) a modern, open, high-N auditory master matrix over the digit vocabulary — the
  same vocabulary as Clarke (1957) Experiment III; (ii) a within-matrix test of the cross-ratio
  condition, which is what Table 5 already reports and rejects at four for four; (iii) a
  Thurstone-vs-Luce fit comparison on a fixed set, since counts are available and the two models make
  different predictions about the *pattern* of off-diagonal cells even without a restriction; (iv) a
  task-transfer test, since recognition and recall matrices exist over the same items.
- The four matrices differ in overall error rate (recognition ~5%, memory ~6.5% but with far more
  trials), giving a modest discriminability contrast.

Highest-value immediate action from this file alone: fit a zero-parameter Gaussian (Thurstone) and a
zero-parameter Gumbel (Luce) model to Tables 1-4 and compare out-of-sample scores. No library, no
paywall, no digitizing.

## What the authors concluded about CRR, quoted verbatim where possible

The authors' own abstract, verbatim and in full:

> "It has been shown that, contrary to current opinion, digits do not confuse arbitrarily and that
> similar patterns of confusion are observed in memory and auditory recognition tasks. Two alternative
> measures of stimulus similarity are compared."

On the CRR specifically, verbatim (p. 380) — note "somewhat dubious", and note that "Morgan, 1973b" is
Morgan (1974), "On Luce's choice axiom":

> "Unless one subscribes to the somewhat dubious (see Cane, 1960; Morgan, 1973b) 'constant ratio rule'
> (CRR) (Clarke, 1957), also known as 'Luce's choice axiom' (Luce, 1959), then these factors will
> influence the predictions of the experiments and confound any comparisons one might wish to make
> between experiments."

On the predicted direction of CRR failure when going from a large master to small vocabularies,
verbatim (p. 382):

> "Thus, prediction from the full alphabetic confusion matrix to small vocabularies using the CRR could
> overestimate the errors for low-confusion subsets such as C F J. The reasons for this would be that,
> with such subsets, the vowel alone gives all the information necessary. When these letters occur with a
> larger vocabulary, the consonants must be coded as well, presumably reducing the efficiency of the vowel
> coding. Thus, the /s/-/f/ relation, for example, could give rise to C-F confusions. There is
> insufficient data in the literature to permit a present test of such hypotheses."

That last sentence — "*There is insufficient data in the literature to permit a present test of such
hypotheses*" — is the 1973 verdict on exactly the question this project is asking, from the author who
had just built the statistical test for it. It is the single best quotation in the branch for
motivating new data collection.

On rejecting the equal-confusability / cross-ratio null, verbatim (p. 376):

> "As x^2_55 (0.1%) = 90, the absurdly high values above imply rejection of H0 in all four cases. This
> was a case when the data were so obviously significant that a statistical test was really unnecessary!"

And their primary conclusion, verbatim (p. 382):

> "Our primary conclusions are that the digits are not equally memorable or recognizable and that
> confusions are systematic."
