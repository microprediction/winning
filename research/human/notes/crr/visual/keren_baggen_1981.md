# Keren & Baggen (1981) — DOES NOT QUALIFY

## Citation

Keren, G., & Baggen, S. (1981). Recognition models of alphanumeric characters.
*Perception & Psychophysics*, 29(3), 234–246.
doi:10.3758/BF03207290
(Institute for Perception TNO, Soesterberg; Institute for Perception Research, Eindhoven.)

Companion exchange in the same journal: Smith, J. E. K. (1982), "Recognition models
evaluated: A commentary on Keren and Baggen", 31, 183–189, doi:10.3758/BF03206219; and
Keren & Baggen (1982), "Recognition models reevaluated: A response to Smith", 31, 192–193,
doi:10.3758/BF03206221. Neither retrieved; both are commentary on model selection, not new
data.

## Stimuli and master response set

**Ten black seven-segment ("segmented") numerals 0–9**, of the kind used on calculator
displays, mounted on white vinyl cards and built from Cello-Tak transfer straight lines
0.012 cm wide. Each numeral 1.8 cm vertical (47 min of arc) × 1.1 cm horizontal (30 min of
arc). Two cards per numeral, one presented 2.6 cm left and one 2.6 cm right of the fixation
cross, i.e. parafoveal presentation.

Scientific Prototype N-1000 three-field tachistoscope, all fields at 4.0 fL. Trial sequence:
fixation cross → 100 ms white blank → stimulus → 100 ms blank → fixation. Exposure duration
titrated per subject to hit 70% correct, ranging from **2 ms to 4.4 ms**.

Eight Erindale College (University of Toronto) undergraduates, two sessions each, 300 trials
per session of which the first 100 were practice — so **200 experimental trials per subject
per session, 3,200 experimental trials in total**. Feedback during practice only. Subjects
could respond "illegible" and did so on under 4% of trials; those trials were discarded.

**Master response set = the ten numerals 0–9, and it never changes.** The authors chose 70%
as the accuracy floor precisely *because* the response set was fixed at ten:
"In order to avoid guessing, it was decided not to set the criterion below 70%, since the
stimulus population (and consequently possible responses) consisted of only 10 items."

## Restricted response sets (nested, overlapping, or a relabelling)

**NONE. The paper does not qualify.**

There is exactly one identification condition and one response set. The paper's comparative
work is across **datasets**, not across response sets within a dataset: it re-analyses the
letter confusion matrix of Gilmore, Hersh, Caramazza & Griffin (1979) and refers to Townsend
(1971) and Van Nes & Bouma (1980), but these are separate experiments with different fonts,
different stimuli and different subjects. Comparing them tests parameter *portability across
studies*, which is a much weaker and more confounded thing than a within-subject
master-to-subset test.

No relabelling either: the digit names are the response labels throughout.

## What numbers are printed (which tables, counts or proportions, per subject or pooled)

- **Table 1 — "Confusion Matrix for Segmented Numerals Obtained From the Experiment."** A
  single 10×10 matrix of **row-conditional proportions to 3 decimals, pooled over all eight
  subjects and over both left and right presentation positions.** Verified: rows sum to
  1.000 (e.g. row 1 = .877+.007+.007+.022+.004+.015+.060+.000+.004+.004 = 1.000).
  Diagonal: .877, .782, .681, .732, .669, .633, .667, .577, .550, .818.
  **Counts are not printed** and are not exactly recoverable: total trials per stimulus is
  approximately 3,200/10 = 320 less the discarded "illegible" trials (<4%, not broken down by
  stimulus), so per-row denominators are known only to within a few percent.
- **Table 2 (a and b)** — fit statistics for the choice model and for the authors' two
  feature-analytic model versions (11 and 19 parameters), applied to their own data (2a) and
  to the Gilmore et al. (1979) letter matrix (2b).
- **Table 3** — the 14-feature list and the feature definition of each letter, used for the
  Gilmore et al. re-analysis.
- **Figure 1** — probability of confusing two digits as a function of the number of
  distinguishing segments, plus the seven-segment numbering.

## Usability verdict (usable now / needs digitizing / needs library access / unusable, and why)

**UNUSABLE for a CRR test — excluded on design.**

- **One response set only.** Nothing to renormalise from or to.
- Table 1 is pooled over 8 subjects and 2 retinal positions, and is given as proportions with
  imprecisely known denominators, so even as a standalone matrix it is weaker than Getty's or
  Townsend & Landon's.

Retained here for two reasons that are worth more than the data:

1. It is a **clean, well-powered 10×10 visual identification matrix at ~70% accuracy** with a
   principled feature description. If the project ever wants a *within-set* Gaussian-vs-Gumbel
   comparison (fit both to the same matrix and compare on held-out cells) rather than an
   across-set forecast, this is a serviceable single matrix, and its seven-segment structure
   gives an independent, non-circular similarity predictor.
2. It contains the sharpest published statement that **the Luce choice model's parameters are
   not invariant**, which is the same failure the CRR makes but observed across studies rather
   than across response sets. See the quotations below.

## What the authors concluded about CRR, quoted verbatim where possible

**The constant-ratio rule is never named** — Clarke, Hodge, Egan, Morgan, Anderson and Lee are
all absent from the reference list. But the paper is an extended critique of Luce's choice
model, and two of its five numbered objections are exactly IIA-invariance objections.

**On bias parameters not being invariant across experiments** — the across-study analogue of
the CRR failure:

> "It is common to assume that response bias may be correlated with the relative frequency of
> occurrence of the stimuli under study, but there has been little explicit discussion of what
> exactly the psychological nature and conceptual meaning of response bias is. If, indeed,
> response bias is, among other things, a function of frequency, then it would be reasonable to
> expect that it should be stable across different experimental situations using the same
> stimuli. In particular, the response bias vectors for different confusion matrices of
> alphabetic characters (even if the type fonts used are somewhat different from condition to
> condition) should be similar. This at least is not the case with regard to the data reported
> by Gilmore et al. (1979) and Townsend (1971), in which the corresponding response bias
> vectors have a quite different pattern."

**On bias not even being consistent across subjects within their own experiment** — note that
this directly contradicts the SCM's standard assumption and echoes Townsend & Landon's finding
that β showed "no systematic variation":

> "Moreover, the response bias is assumed to be common across subjects; we have carefully
> inspected the individual data on the basis of which Table 1 was constructed, and were unable
> to reveal any salient response bias across subjects. Rather, it seems as if the pattern of
> biases (if it exists) is unique to each individual."

Restated in the Results:

> "Finally, it is important to notice that inspection of the individual data did not reveal
> consistent response biases across subjects."

**On bias and similarity being confounded**, so that the decomposition the choice model
promises is not identified:

> "...and noticed a correlation of -.733 between the response bias vector and the average
> similarity (for each stimulus) produced by the choice model."

> "Goldstein (Note 1), citing Holman, has shown that any set of data that can be modeled by
> using two bias components (one for stimulus and one for response) can be modeled equally well
> by using just a single response bias. Thus, Goldstein (Note 1) argued that 'mathematically
> there is no unique solution, in the metric model, for representing the asymmetry of the matrix
> in terms of bias functions'."

**On the choice model's parameter count — the parsimony objection, which is precisely the
argument for a zero-parameter contest:**

> "...eters have to be estimated in order to predict the 676 cells of the confusion matrix. The
> large number of parameters estimated by the choice model probably accounts for its superior
> performance, but obviously there is still a lot to be desired in terms of parsimony. The
> final outcome of the model is an estimated similarity matrix (which may be accurate or not),
> but little insight is gained with regard to the underlying similarity structure."

> "There are two general criteria on which to test the quality of a model: One is its
> predictability or goodness of fit, that is, how close the predictions are to the observed
> data. However, one may always increase the number of parameters to be estimated and
> consequently gain additional precision. The second criterion is parsimony and the explanatory
> power of the model... While the choice model performs excellently in terms of the first
> criterion, it does rather poorly in terms of the second one."

**On a systematic asymmetry in visual confusions** — a directional regularity that a symmetric
similarity model cannot capture, worth noting as a second class of systematic residual:

> "It is, indeed, more often the case that, with highly similar letter pairs, the letter that
> contains more segments is more frequently confused with the letter that contains fewer
> segments than vice versa. For instance, E is more frequently confused with F than conversely,
> as indicated from the data reported by Townsend (1971). However, inspection of existing data
> (e.g., confusion matrices of letters and numerals) suggests that the rule cannot be stated
> deterministically."

Though in their own data they could not sign it consistently:

> "First, the confusion matrix is obviously not symmetrical. Moreover, it is difficult to
> generalize the direction of asymmetry: In some instances, it is more often the case that
> numerals with the larger number of segments are confused with numerals consisting of fewer
> segments than vice versa (e.g., 7-1, 8-0, 4-1). In other instances, however, the order is
> reversed (e.g., 6-8, 3-9, 5-9)."
