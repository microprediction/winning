AFCRC = **Air Force Cambridge Research Center**; the TN-58- prefix indicates the report was
numbered in the 1958 series and issued/dated 1959. The Hearing and Communication Laboratory
at Indiana University is Egan's and Clarke's lab — the same lab that produced Clarke (1957),
Clarke & Anderson (1957) and Egan's Technical Report 57-50. C. D. Anderson is the Anderson of
Clarke & Anderson (1957), *J. Acoust. Soc. Am.* 29, 1318–1320.

## Stimuli and master response set

**Visually presented monosyllables.** This is the one hard fact about the stimuli, and it is
what puts this report in the visual branch rather than the auditory one. Townsend & Landon
state it explicitly while claiming priority for their own study:

> "It has been employed or tested in other contexts (e.g., auditory recognition; Clarke,
> 1957) but not, to our knowledge, in visual letter recognition (Anderson, Note 3, employed
> visually presented monosyllables)."

So: tachistoscopic (hence "brief exposure duration") presentation of monosyllabic words or
syllables, with the master response set presumably the full word/syllable ensemble. Set sizes,
ensemble composition, number of subjects and trial counts are all **unknown**.

The title's phrase "among various stimuli" suggests more than one stimulus type or more than
one ensemble, but this cannot be confirmed without the document.

## Restricted response sets (nested, overlapping, or a relabelling)

**Presumed present but unverified.** The inference rests on three points:

1. The title frames the CRR as "a **predictor** of confusions" — in this literature CRR is
   *only* ever used as a predictor of a restricted-set matrix from a master matrix (Clarke
   1957, Definition 1). A paper testing CRR as a predictor necessarily has at least two
   response sets over the same stimuli.
2. Townsend & Landon list Anderson among the studies that "have been conducted, and most have
   concluded that the CRR does make satisfactory predictions with choice data (Anderson,
   Note 3; Clarke, 1957, 1959; Clarke & Anderson, 1957; Egan, 1957, Note 4; Hodge, 1967;
   Hodge & Pollack, 1962; Pollack & Decker, 1960)." Every other member of that list uses a
   master-plus-subset design.
3. The sibling paper from the same lab and author, Clarke & Anderson (1957), is titled
   "Further tests of the constant-ratio rule in speech communication" and uses Clarke's
   nested-subset design.

Whether the subsets are nested, overlapping, or a relabelling is **unknown**.

## What numbers are printed (which tables, counts or proportions, per subject or pooled)

**Unknown.** No copy has been obtained.

Prior probability, based on the conventions of its cohort: AFCRC technical notes from this
laboratory were typically generous with tabulated matrices (they were deliverables under an
Air Force contract, not page-limited journal articles), so this report is more likely than the
journal literature of the period to print full master and subset matrices. Against that,
Clarke (1957) and Egan (1957) both **pooled over subjects**, and Morgan (1974) showed that
pooling was what made their CRR conclusions look favourable — so if Anderson followed lab
practice, per-subject matrices are unlikely.

## Usability verdict (usable now / needs digitizing / needs library access / unusable, and why)

**NEEDS LIBRARY / ARCHIVE ACCESS — currently blocked, but not hopeless, and worth one more
attempt later.**

Rationale for continuing to chase it:
- It is chronologically the **first** CRR test on visual stimuli, predating Hodge (1967) by
  eight years and Townsend & Landon (1982) by twenty-three.
- As a technical report rather than a journal article it is the item in this literature most
  likely to print full confusion matrices, which is exactly what Hodge (1967) failed to do.
- It is the only visual-branch item whose data status is genuinely unknown; everything else in
  this directory has been resolved one way or the other.

Rationale for not blocking on it:
- Two fully usable datasets already exist (Townsend & Landon 1982; Getty et al. 1979), and
  between them they cover both the nested-subset and the restricted-response-set forms of the
  test with per-subject counts.
- Even in the best case Anderson's data are likely pooled over subjects, which limits their
  evidential value for the same reason Morgan (1974) discounted Clarke and Egan.
- The single hard blocker (DTIC being offline) is outside our control and time-limited.

## What the authors concluded about CRR, quoted verbatim where possible

**Not available — no copy obtained. No verbatim quotation from Anderson is possible.**

What can be established is how Anderson's conclusion was *received*, from two secondary
sources both read in full:

Townsend & Landon (1982) place Anderson in the pro-CRR camp and then immediately undercut the
whole group on statistical grounds:

> "Several tests of the CRR have been conducted, and most have concluded that the CRR does
> make satisfactory predictions with choice data (Anderson, Note 3; Clarke, 1957, 1959;
> Clarke & Anderson, 1957; Egan, 1957, Note 4; Hodge, 1967; Hodge & Pollack, 1962; Pollack &
> Decker, 1960). However, there is some question as to the validity of the results of these
> past studies due to what was at the time a lack of any statistical test of the deviations of
> the results from the predictions of the CRR."

and later:

> "Although the CRR has been found to perform fairly well, the previous tests of the CRR were
> not very stringent in that they lacked strict statistical tests of CRR predictions."

Hodge (1967) cites Anderson only in the opening survey of prior CRR tests, alongside speech
stimuli, words and multidimensional tones, and it is Hodge's parenthetical
"visually presented monosyllables (Anderson, 1959)" that identifies the modality:

> "A number of tests has shown that the CRR makes satisfactory predictions of the confusion
> patterns associated with multidimensional objects such as various speech stimuli (Clarke,
> 1957; Clarke & Anderson, 1957; Carterette & Wyman, 1961; Pollack & Decker, 1960), words
> (Egan, 1957), visually presented monosyllables (Anderson, 1959), and multidimensional
> auditory tones (Hodge & Pollack, 1962)."

So Anderson (1959) is reported by both secondary sources as **favourable to the CRR**, and by
Townsend & Landon as favourable for a reason that would not survive a modern test: no
statistical assessment of the deviations. Given Hodge's and Townsend & Landon's later findings
of a signed, systematic residual under exactly this kind of design, a "satisfactory
predictions" verdict from 1959 should be read as "the residual was there and was not looked
for."
