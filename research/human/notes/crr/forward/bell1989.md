# Bell, Dirks & Kincaid 1989 — Closed-set effects in consonant confusion patterns

## Citation

Bell, T. S., Dirks, D. D. and Kincaid, G. E. (1989). "Closed-Set Effects in Consonant Confusion
Patterns." *Journal of Speech and Hearing Research* 32(4): 944-948.
doi:10.1044/jshr.3204.944. All three authors, Department of Surgery, UCLA.

**This is the full paper behind the assigned candidate**, which was the meeting abstract:
Bell, T. S., Dirks, D. D. and Kincaid, G. E. (1987). "Evaluation of the constant ratio rule for
consonant confusions." *JASA* 81(S1): S3. doi:10.1121/1.2024227, OpenAlex W1997217812 — a
one-paragraph Acoustical Society meeting abstract, not an article. The two describe the same
experiment (same three authors, same 14 consonants, same three intensity levels, same
male/female talker pair, same log-linear analysis); the 1987 abstract is the one that names the
constant-ratio rule in its title, the 1989 paper is where the work was published.

Related but distinct, same group, **not** restricted-set studies: Bell, Dirks & Carterette
(1986) "Log-linear modeling of consonant confusion data", *JASA* 79(2): 518-525,
doi:10.1121/1.393539; and Bell, Dirks & Carterette (1989) "Interactive factors in consonant
confusion patterns", *JASA* 85(1): 339-346, doi:10.1121/1.397685.

## Domain and stimuli

Auditory / speech. Normal-hearing young adults identifying **digitized vowel-consonant (VC)
syllables**, each of 14 consonants (stops and fricatives, voiced and voiceless) paired with the
vowel **/a/**, spoken by **1 male and 1 female talker**, presented at **three intensity levels**.
The stimulus set is fixed at 14 consonants throughout; what varies is the response set offered
to the listener.

## Master and restricted response sets

Yes — the manipulated variable *is* the closed response set, which is unusual and exactly what
the project needs. Verbatim from the 1989 abstract:

> "Invariance of error patterns in confusion matrices of varying dimensions were examined.
> Normal-hearing young adults were presented closed-set arrangements of digitized syllable
> tokens, spoken by 1 male and 1 female talker, and selected from a set of 14 consonants (stops
> and fricatives)."

and

> "Patterns of errors among voiceless stops and among voiced fricatives were dependent on the
> set of alternatives. Voiceless fricatives and voiced stops were not significantly affected by
> the number of response alternatives."

So: multiple closed-set *arrangements* of differing *dimension* over a common 14-consonant pool,
with results explicitly analysed as a function of (a) the identity/composition of the response
alternatives ("the set of alternatives", called "the nature of the response foil" in the 1987
abstract) and (b) the *number* of response alternatives. That is a menu-composition test and a
menu-size test in the same design.

**UNVERIFIED:** whether one of the arrangements is the full 14-alternative master menu, and
what the exact subset compositions and sizes are. The abstracts say "varying dimensions" and
"number of response alternatives" without listing them. It is possible the design is a family of
overlapping medium-sized subsets with no single all-14 condition, in which case the project's
calibrate-on-full-menu protocol would have to be re-cast as calibrate-on-largest-menu.

## What numbers are printed or deposited

**UNVERIFIED — I could not obtain the article body.** What is known:

- The analysis is **log-linear modelling**, not raw-matrix presentation. This group's method
  paper (Bell, Dirks & Carterette 1986, *JASA* 79: 518-525) fits log-linear models to consonant
  confusion tables and reports parameter estimates and goodness-of-fit tests. A 5-page JSHR
  paper (944-948) using that machinery on a 14-consonant pool across 2 talkers x 3 levels x
  several closed-set arrangements has room for model-parameter tables and significance tests but
  **very little room for the raw matrices themselves** — a single 14x14 alone is 196 cells.
  My working expectation is therefore that the paper prints fitted interaction parameters and
  ANOVA-style tests, not the observed matrices.
- The 1987 meeting abstract prints no numbers at all (it is a single paragraph on page S3).
- No data deposit (1989); JSHR of that era had no supplementary-materials mechanism.
- The 1987 abstract does state the qualitative pattern in a way that is directly relevant:
  relationships among voiceless stop consonants stayed constant "irrespective of the nature of
  the response foil", while other consonant classes depended on the alternatives available —
  i.e. a **partial, class-specific IIA violation**, with some sub-blocks of the matrix obeying
  proportional renormalisation and others not.

If the raw matrices are not in the paper, the fallback is that the log-linear parameter
estimates for the response-set-by-error-pattern interaction constitute a published, signed
measure of IIA violation that a Thurstone model's predictions could be qualitatively checked
against — much weaker than cell-level scoring, but it is a real, statistically tested result
rather than an eyeball claim.

## Usability verdict

**Design is right; the printed numbers are almost certainly not sufficient, and are UNVERIFIED.**
This is the only candidate in the auditory/speech cluster that manipulates response-set
*composition* as well as *size*, and the only one that reports a **differential** result — some
consonant classes obey the constant-ratio rule across response foils and others do not. That
makes it the most interesting result in the cluster conceptually. But it is a log-linear
modelling paper in five journal pages, so cell-level observed matrices for a 14-consonant pool
crossed with 2 talkers and 3 levels cannot plausibly all be printed, and no data were deposited.
Without the raw matrices there is nothing to calibrate a Gaussian model on.

## Conclusion about CRR quoted verbatim

The authors' conclusion as stated in the 1989 published abstract (p. 944), quoted verbatim from
the Crossref record:

> "Patterns of errors among voiceless stops and among voiced fricatives were dependent on the
> set of alternatives. Voiceless fricatives and voiced stops were not significantly affected by
> the number of response alternatives. Speaker differences, individual differences among
> listeners, and implications relating to the generalization of confusion data collected in
> small closed-set arrangements arc discussed."

["arc" is the publisher's own OCR typo for "are", present in the Crossref record.]

And from the 1987 *JASA* meeting abstract (p. S3), as returned by OpenAlex: the relationships
among voiceless stop consonants stayed "constant irrespective of the nature of the response
foil", while patterns for other consonant classes depended on the available alternatives.
