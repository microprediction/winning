# Engstrand & Moeller (1967)

## Citation

Engstrand, R.D., & Moeller, G. (1967). Confusion matrix analysis for form perception.
*Human Factors*, 9(5), 439–446. DOI 10.1177/001872086700900507. PMID 5582459.
Submarine Medical Research Laboratory, Naval Submarine Medical Center, Naval Submarine Base
New London, Groton, Connecticut. Also issued as DTIC report AD0668614.

## Domain and stimuli

Visual **form perception** — tachistoscopic identification of shapes. Half the master set is
**non-letter**: 10 simple geometric figures (circle, triangle, semi-circle, pentagon,
rectangle, ellipse, arrow, cross, star, zig-zag). The other half is upper-case English
letters, which lets the paper compare a letter subset against a geometric-figure subset
under identical CRR arithmetic — useful, because the letter/non-letter contrast is where the
rule breaks.

Three experiments in two studies.

- Experiment I (Study I, 12 subjects): master set of **36 stimuli** = 26 upper-case letters
  + 10 geometric figures. Crossed 2 (brightness contrast: 2.2% vs higher) x 2 (mode: light
  figures on dark surround vs dark on light). Each subject in one mode, both contrasts; 8
  responses per brightness contrast per stimulus over four days.
- Experiment II (Study I, 28 subjects): **20-stimulus** set = 10 of those letters + the same
  10 geometric figures, two brightness contrasts (3.2% and 2.5%), dark figures on light
  surround.
- Experiment III (Study II, 10 subjects): the same 20-element set and the **10-element
  geometric-figure subset**, all conditions held constant except set size; 40 ms exposures,
  Scientific Prototype Model G tachistoscope, verbal responses using the international
  phonetic alphabet for letters and descriptive names for figures. 50 presentations per
  character in the 10-condition, 25 in the 20-condition; 2000 observations per subject.

All subjects were submarine-school candidates with normal or corrected-to-normal acuity.
Multiple-choice task with a visible recognition field listing the possible responses;
subjects instructed to guess when uncertain. Occasional non-responses were logged as "blank".

## Master response set and restricted response sets (nested, overlapping, or a relabelling)

This is a textbook **nested** master-matrix / submatrix design and it is stated as such:

- 36 x 36 master matrix (Experiment I) → CRR-predicted **20 x 20** submatrix, tested against
  the empirically obtained 20 x 20 matrix of Experiment II.
- 36-element and 20-element data → CRR-predicted **10 x 10** submatrices for the 10-letter
  subset and the 10-geometric-figure subset. No empirical 10 x 10 data in Study I.
- Study II supplies the missing empirical **10 x 10** geometric-figure matrix under
  conditions otherwise identical to its own 20-element master matrix — the cleanest single
  test in the paper, because only set size differs.
- The 20-element master matrix is common to Experiment II and Experiment III, giving a direct
  empirical test-retest check (r = .64) that bounds how well any rule could do.

Because non-responses break the square-matrix assumption, they ran the whole analysis three
ways: dropping blanks, adding a dummy "blank" response column (36 x 37 and 20 x 21), and a
side experiment presenting a blank slide as a stimulus to force squareness. All three agreed.

Comparison counts: 36 within-experiment comparisons in each of Experiments I and II, 81
across experiments, 153 total for the 20 x 20 level; 306 correlations at the 10 x 10 level.

## What numbers are printed (which tables, counts or proportions, per subject or pooled)

Pooled/summary proportions, not raw counts, and only the correct-response diagonal:

- **Table 1** prints derived proportions of correct response for the 10 letters and the 10
  figures common to both experiments, four columns: letters-from-36-element data,
  letters-from-20-element data, figures-from-36-element data, figures-from-20-element data.
  E.g. Delta .97/.81, Victor .95/.76, Kilo .92/.71, November .91/.63, Whiskey .89/.77,
  Lima .86/.70, Zulu .86/.67, Bravo .78/.52, Juliett .69/.72, Hotel .64/.56; Circle .98/.94,
  Triangle .96/.82, Semi-circle .94/.74, Pentagon .88/.76, Rectangle .83/.64, Ellipse
  .76/.72, Arrow .68/.51, Cross .67/.52, Star .66/.52, Zig-zag .64/.59.
- Figure 2: scatter of obtained vs CRR-derived correct-response proportions for the 20 x 20
  matrix; fitted line y = .79x - .02, r = .81, slope not significantly different from unity.
- Figure 3: obtained vs predicted for the Study II 10-element geometric subset; fitted
  y = 1.009x - .003, Pearson r = .99 overall and r = .83 for correct responses only (p < .01).
- Figure 4: empirical Experiment II vs empirical Experiment III for the common 20-element
  master matrix; y = .76x + .08, r = .64 (p < .01).
- Rank-order agreement between the two derivations in Table 1: rho_letters = +.617 (p < .05),
  rho_figures = +.918 (p < .01).

**The confusion matrices themselves are not printed.** They are explicit about discarding the
off-diagonal mass from the analysis ("the bulk of the values from each confusion matrix, the
elements lying in the triangular matrices above and below the negative diagonal, have been
omitted purposefully"), and the full intercorrelation tables were **deposited with the
American Documentation Institute** as Document No. ADI9671 rather than printed — a 1967
microfilm deposit service that no longer exists in that form.

## Usability verdict (usable now / needs digitizing / needs library access / unusable, and why)

**Usable now, but only for the diagonal.** The PDF is free and complete, and Table 1's forty
derived correct-response proportions plus the three scatter-plot regressions are directly
transcribable — enough to reproduce their CRR test at the level of P(correct), and enough to
document the shrinking-subset overprediction bias with real numbers.

It is **not** usable as a confusion-matrix source. The 36 x 36, 20 x 20 and 10 x 10 matrices
are never printed, the off-diagonal cells were deliberately excluded from the analysis, and
the supporting intercorrelation tables went to an ADI microfilm deposit (Document No.
ADI9671) that would now require tracing through the Library of Congress / NTIS successor to
the American Documentation Institute deposit programme — low odds, high effort. Treat the
raw matrices as lost unless the Naval Submarine Medical Research Laboratory report series
retained them.

Value to this project: it is the clearest printed statement in the CRR literature that the
rule **overpredicts the diagonal as the subset shrinks relative to the master set**, and it
gives an arithmetic account of why (denominator estimation error grows as cells are dropped).
That is a systematic, direction-signed failure of proportional renormalization, not noise —
and it is the same direction Hodge (1967) reports.

## What the authors concluded, quoted verbatim where possible

Abstract, verbatim:

> "The Constant-Ratio Rule (CRR), an empirical technique for analysis of confusion matrices,
> was developed for use in predicting intelligibility of speech syllables. This study
> investigated the validity of the rule when applied to the data from experiments on visual
> form perception. English letters and simple geometric figures were tachistoscopically
> presented in the center of a viewing field. Response proportions for subsets of this
> master set of stimuli were predicted by CRR. Results indicated that the rule (1)
> accurately predicted numeric response proportions for subsets of stimuli when experimental
> conditions were similar and (2) predicted ordinally accurate data when experimental
> conditions varied within the limit which might be encountered in 'operational situations.'
> These results, as well as arithmetic factors which can result in errors in prediction, are
> discussed."

Study I summary:

> "Thus the data of Study I demonstrates that: (1) response proportions for a 20 element
> matrix predicted from the data of a 36 element matrix compare favorably with the
> empirically obtained proportions of responses to the same set of stimuli presented under
> different brightness conditions and to different subjects; (2) response probabilities for
> 10 x 10 submatrices predicted from data collected under different sets of experimental
> conditions produced similar rank orderings of stimulus discriminability. Discrepancies in
> the predicted response proportions began to appear when CRR was applied to the data for
> alphabetic figures obtained under the different conditions of experiment I and II. The
> reasons for these discrepancies could not be determined from the data of this study."

The shrinking-subset bias — the most useful sentence in the paper for this project:

> "Examination of Table 1 also shows that values derived from experiment I for the 10 element
> subset are larger than the corresponding values derived from the data of experiment II.
> This apparent tendency of the rule to overpredict, as size of subset (relative to size of
> master matrix) decreases, has been observed by other investigators."

Their arithmetic explanation:

> "All arguments have, of course, concluded that an empirical rule which does not provide for
> changes in parameters with changes in conditions cannot possibly make numerically accurate
> predictions." ... "the greater the discrepancy between the number of cells in the master
> matrix and the number of cells in the submatrix, the greater should be the error in the
> estimate of the 'true' value of the denominator. If the number of observations in each row
> is small, then low probability events are unlikely to have occurred. Consequently the high
> frequency events will provide the major contribution to the denominator. In the case of
> estimating the probability of a correct response this factor will result in overprediction.
> In the case of estimating the probability of an incorrect response this factor will result
> in underprediction."

Final verdict, hedged in favour of the rule as an engineering tool:

> "Within the limits of this study, CRR was found to be of positive value for the prediction
> of response patterns to visual form stimuli which were to be presented under a variety of
> stimulus conditions and viewed by different samples of subjects. The rule accurately
> predicted exact numeric values when experimental conditions were held constant. When
> experimental conditions were allowed to vary, the rule did not accurately predict numeric
> values, but the use of the rule did provide sufficient information to enable the selection
> of a subset of stimuli from a larger set which sufficiently satisfied pre-established
> criteria and with far less manipulation of data than other existing techniques."

Note the framing: the rule survives only when nothing changes, and it degrades ordinally
when anything does. Contrast with their own concession that "Considering the stimuli used in
this investigation together with the inherent variability associated with responses to these
stimuli, CRR did about as well as we could have expected" — a null result defended by
appeal to low test-retest reliability (r = .64), exactly the power objection Wills et al.
(2000) raise against the pro-CRR literature.
