# Bensemann, Lobb, Podlesnik & Elliffe (2015)

## Citation

Bensemann, J., Lobb, B., Podlesnik, C.A., & Elliffe, D. (2015). Steady-state choice between
four alternatives obeys the constant-ratio rule. *Journal of the Experimental Analysis of
Behavior*, 104(1), 7–19. DOI 10.1002/jeab.157. PMID 25989016. University of Auckland.

The deliberate steady-state control for Elliffe & Davison (2010) — see elliffe2010.md. The two
papers must be read and cited as a pair; separately, each is misleading.

## Domain and stimuli

**Operant choice in pigeons** — four-alternative concurrent variable-interval schedules. Not
speech, not letters, not memory. Included as the boundary condition on the only clean CRR
violation found outside categorization.

Six pigeons. Four-alternative concurrent schedule, arranged reinforcer-rate ratio
**27:9:3:1** — the same ratio as Elliffe & Davison (2010). The critical difference is
temporal: the same four VI schedules were used in every condition and held for **50 sessions**,
with only the physical location of each schedule changed across conditions. Elliffe & Davison
changed the location assignment every **10 reinforcers**.

## Master response set and restricted response sets (nested, overlapping, or a relabelling)

Same structure as Elliffe & Davison (2010): nested by extraction, not by menu manipulation.
All four keys are continuously available; nothing is removed. The CRR test asks whether
pairwise preference between two of the four alternatives depends only on those two
alternatives' reinforcer rates, or also on the absolute rates — i.e. on the rest of the choice
set.

Here it **does not** depend on the rest of the set: responding was consistent with the
constant-ratio rule.

The design is therefore a controlled contrast on a single variable — rate of environmental
change — holding the alternative set, the reinforcer ratio, the species, the apparatus and
even the subject count fixed. That is what makes the pair valuable: the CRR is not simply true
or false for four-alternative choice; **it holds at asymptote and fails while the structure is
being learned.**

The local analysis sharpens this. In the steady-state procedure, preference immediately after
a reinforcer was toward the alternative **likely to produce the next reinforcer**; in the
frequently-changing procedure it was toward the **just-reinforced** alternative. Same physical
choice set, different internal use of the reinforcer signal.

## What numbers are printed (which tables, counts or proportions, per subject or pooled)

Not verified beyond the abstract. What the abstract establishes is present, in kind:

- Steady-state (extended) preference measures, generalized-matching-law fits across
  conditions, with the CRR as the tested assumption of that law.
- A direct comparison of the steady-state data against "data from equivalent, frequently
  changing procedures" — i.e. against the Elliffe & Davison (2010) dataset.
- **Local analyses** of preference as a function of time since reinforcement, distinguishing
  preference for the just-reinforced alternative from preference for the next-likely
  alternative.

No confusion matrices in the identification sense. Underlying data are per-key response and
reinforcer counts per condition per pigeon. Six subjects across conditions of 50 sessions
each, so per-animal parameter tables are very likely printed; JEAB house style favours
individual-subject data.

## Usability verdict (usable now / needs digitizing / needs library access / unusable, and why)

**Needs library access** for the numbers; **usable now** as a citation, since the abstract
states the result and the design contrast explicitly.

Priority: **low as a data source, high as a caveat.** The data are pigeon key-peck counts and
will not transfer. But this paper is the reason not to cite Elliffe & Davison (2010) as a
free-standing "CRR is false" result, and the reason the CRR literature's mixed record is not
simply a matter of statistical power. Whoever writes the related-work paragraph needs this
paper in hand, or at least this note.

The one substantive reason to get the full text: the local-preference analysis is the mechanism
claim, and it is the interesting one for this project. Under rapid change, choice is driven by
"reinforcer-generated signals" — the last outcome — and the constant-ratio rule fails; under
stability, choice reflects the learned structure, and it holds. If the analogy to a model
answering a menu question survives scrutiny, this is where the empirical shape of it lives.

## What the authors concluded, quoted verbatim where possible

Full abstract, verbatim:

> "We investigated why violations to the constant-ratio rule, an assumption of the generalized
> matching law, occur in procedures that arrange frequent changes to reinforcer ratios. Our
> investigation produced steady-state data and compared them with data from equivalent,
> frequently changing procedures. Six pigeons responded in a four-alternative concurrent-schedule
> experiment with an arranged reinforcer-rate ratio of 27:9:3:1. The same four variable-interval
> schedules were used in every condition, for 50 sessions, and the physical location of each
> schedule was changed across conditions. The experiment was a steady-state version of a
> frequently changing procedure in which the locations of four VI schedules were changed every 10
> reinforcers. We found that subjects' responding was consistent with the constant-ratio rule in
> the steady-state procedure. Additionally, local analyses showed that preference after
> reinforcement was towards the alternative that was likely to produce the next reinforcer,
> instead of being towards the just-reinforced alternative as in frequently changing procedures.
> This suggests that the effect of a reinforcer on preference is fundamentally different in
> rapidly changing and steady-state environments. Comparing this finding to the existing
> literature suggests that choice is more influenced by reinforcer-generated signals when the
> reinforcement contingencies often change."

The two operative sentences: "We found that subjects' responding was consistent with the
constant-ratio rule in the steady-state procedure" and "This suggests that the effect of a
reinforcer on preference is fundamentally different in rapidly changing and steady-state
environments."

Note the framing of the opening sentence — the constant-ratio rule is described not as a
freestanding hypothesis but as "an assumption of the generalized matching law." That is the
same rhetorical position the rule occupies in the models Wills et al. (2000) attack: an
unexamined default inside a larger theory, adopted for convenience rather than tested.
