# Lee (1968) — detection theory vs micromatching vs CRR: the Gaussian-versus-Luce calculation, done in 1968 with no data

## Citation

Lee, W. (1968). Detection theory, micromatching, and the constant-ratio rule. *Perception &
Psychophysics*, **4**(4), 217-219. doi:10.3758/BF03206305
University of California, Berkeley (author's note: "*Now at Bell Telephone Laboratories, Holmdel, New
Jersey*"). Accepted 10 June 1968. Supported by NIH Grant 11128.

**Citation correction for the brief:** the task listed this as *Psychonomic Science*. It is
*Perception & Psychophysics* 1968, Vol. 4 (4), 217-219. The DOI is correct.

## Stimuli and master response set

**None. There are no stimuli and no subjects.** This is a pure calculation paper. What plays the role
of the master set is a hypothetical set of **three stimuli**, each associated with a normal (Gaussian)
distribution of internal effect, in three geometries (Lee's Fig. 1):

- **Univariate**: three equally spaced normal densities on a single continuum.
- **Bivariate "symmetric"**: three bivariate normal means at the vertices of an equilateral triangle.
- **Bivariate "orthogonal"**: three bivariate normal means at the vertices of an isosceles right
  triangle.

All standard deviations equal across stimuli and dimensions; zero covariance; equal presentation
probabilities. Conditional response probabilities obtained by numerical integration.

## Restricted response sets (state whether nested, overlapping, or a relabelling)

**Nested, hypothetically: every 2-subset of the 3-set.** Verbatim: "*All the cases considered concern
sets of three stimuli and subsets of two of these stimuli.*" Cutoffs are relocated optimally for each
set size — three regions for the triple, a single midpoint cutoff for each pair.

So the paper computes, exactly, what a Gaussian (Thurstonian / signal-detection) mechanism predicts
about the CRR when an alternative is removed, and separately what a "micromatching" mechanism (Lee's
probabilistic sample-to-response mapping, in which response probability at a sample point equals the
posterior probability of each stimulus at that point) predicts.

## What numbers are printed (master matrix? restricted matrices? counts or proportions? which table numbers?)

**Table 1** ("Constancies for Detection Theory (DT) and Micromatching (MM)") is the entire empirical
content, and it is transcribed here in full because it is the historical precursor of the present
project's central comparison.

Lee's "constancy" is defined as
`c_ij = (r_i/r_j in the 2-subset) / (r_i'/r_j' in the 3-set)`,
standardised so that c <= 1.0. **The CRR predicts c = 1.0 exactly.** Six cases; d' is the distance
between the two subset means in units of the common standard deviation.

## Usability verdict (usable now / needs digitizing / needs library access / unusable, and why)

**USABLE NOW as theory; UNUSABLE as data.** There is no experiment here — no stimuli, no subjects, no
confusion matrix — so it cannot enter a forecasting benchmark. Obtained in full, so nothing further
is needed.

Read it as the project's direct ancestor and cite it as such. Two concrete uses:

1. **It quantifies exactly where Gaussian and Luce renormalization diverge.** Lee's case (2)
   (`r1/r3` conditional on `s1`, univariate) is the killer: constancy falls monotonically from 1.00 at
   d'=0 to **0.22 at d'=3** under detection theory. That is a factor-of-4.5 violation of the CRR
   predicted by pure Gaussian machinery with no free parameters. Conversely case (1) rises to 1.00. So a
   Gaussian model predicts *patterned, signed, d'-dependent* violations of IIA — precisely the kind of
   prediction a zero-parameter forecasting contest can score, and precisely what the 1957-1974
   literature never tested.
2. **It explains the single-vs-multidimensional split** that Hodge & Pollack (1962) and Hodge (1967)
   found empirically: constancy holds up better in the bivariate cases than the univariate ones.

## What the authors concluded about CRR, quoted verbatim where possible

Lee's abstract, verbatim and in full:

> "The conformance of detection theory and micromatching with the constant-ratio rule (CRR) was
> determined by calculations for sets of three stimuli and subsets of two stimuli. For an arbitrary
> selection of sets, the CRR would be expected to hold quite well if either detection theory or
> micromatching lay behind the responses. For specific stimulus configurations, however, detection
> theory and micromatching differ considerably from one another and from the CRR. These configurations
> are found particularly for the univariate distributions, and this corresponds to empirical findings
> that the CRR holds better for multidimensional than for unidimensional stimuli. It is shown how the
> differences between the three theories in the implied constancies of ratios can be used in diagnosis
> of the basis of empirical confusion matrices."

On the priority and identity of the rule, verbatim (p. 217):

> "Luce (1959) treated the problem without assuming distributions associated with each signal.
> According to an implication of his axiom I, when signal si is presented, the ratio of the response
> probabilities conditional on that signal, rj/rk (where j or k might equal i), has a value that is
> constant regardless of the presence or absence of any signals other than i, j, and k that might be
> included in the signal set. Clarke (1957) independently proposed this same hypothesis and called it
> the constant-ratio-rule (CRR)."

On the evidential base for Luce's axiom, verbatim (p. 217) — note that the whole of Luce's empirical
support was Clarke (1957):

> "Clarke (1957) published results utilizing spoken syllables, words, and numbers, with highly
> favorable results. Luce (1959) took these results as the only findings bearing on the adequacy of his
> axiom I and considered the results to be highly favorable to his theory. Later studies have been
> somewhat less consistent."

On how to design a decisive test, verbatim (p. 219):

> "It seems clear from the table that if one were selecting cases arbitrarily, the CRR could be
> expected to hold quite well whether detection theory or micromatching were in actuality behind the
> responses. If one wished for a critical test, however, one would not choose cases arbitrarily, but
> would choose cases where the prediction of the CRR, detection theory, and micromatching differ
> considerably. These would be univariate cases, since here is where the notable differences occur..."

And the plea that the field ignored, verbatim (p. 219) — worth quoting in the paper, because it is the
1968 statement of exactly the data-availability problem this project is up against:

> "In view of the implied effects of the individual stimulus configurations on constancy, I would
> suggest that investigators of the CRR give more detail on individual response probabilities for
> specific configurations, rather than simply giving gross plots and gross statistics. If this level of
> detail is not possible for a journal article, at least the availability of the data could be
> advertized."
