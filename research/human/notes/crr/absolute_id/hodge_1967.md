## Citation

Hodge, M. H. (1967). Some further tests of the constant-ratio rule. *Perception &
Psychophysics*, 2(10), 429-437. DOI 10.3758/BF03208790. Author and pagination confirmed via
Crossref. University of Georgia.

## Domain and stimuli

Five experiments on unidimensional stimuli in three modalities: lifted weights
(medicine bottles on a turntable, spaced 6 g apart in Exp 2), visual size (circles, areas
from 50.24 sq in.), and visual brightness. Ensembles of 2, 4 or 8 stimulus objects.
Interstimulus spacing and ensemble range were varied across series, and practice effects
were tracked.

## Master response set and restricted response sets (nested, overlapping, or a relabelling)

**Genuinely nested, and unidimensional — exactly the configuration we care about.**
Hodge states the structure in Luce's terms:

> "given two sets of well defined stimuli and responses, with one S-R set a subset of the
> other, knowledge of the choice probabilities for one set permits prediction of the
> probabilities for the other set."

Master 8x8 matrices, with 4x4 and 2x2 submatrices drawn from them. The spacing manipulation
is nested too: "the first pair of stimuli in Series D (numbered from the top and designated
D 1-2) is the same pair of circles as B 1-3 and A 1-5", so the same physical pair recurs
with different numbers of intervening stimuli — a built-in range/density control.

## What numbers are printed or deposited (which tables/files, counts or proportions, per subject or pooled)

**Summary statistics only. No matrices.** This is the disappointment of the branch.
Table 1 ("Summary of Results of Lifted Weights Experiments") gives, per matrix size and per
half of the rank-ordered matrices: mean proportion correct, mean absolute difference,
percentage of differences exceeding .10, and the *signed* mean diagonal difference. Figures
1 and 3 are scatterplots of obtained against CRR-predicted proportions for individual cells,
so the cells exist as ink but not as numbers.

The signed diagonal differences are the recoverable residual, and they are systematic:
for the 8-step lifted-weights experiment, +.017 (8x8), +.004 (4x4 first half), +.030 (4x4
second half), +.044, -.011, +.099 (2x2 second half) — the deviation grows as the matrix
shrinks and as accuracy falls.

Wayne Lee, writing in this literature shortly afterwards, complained about exactly this
practice: "I would suggest that investigators of the CRR give more detail on individual
response probabilities for specific configurations, rather than simply giving gross plots
and gross statistics. If this level of detail is not possible for a journal article, at
least the availability of the data could be advertized." See `lee_1970.md`.

## Usability verdict (usable now / needs digitizing / needs library access / unusable, and why)

**Unusable as a scoring target** — nested unidimensional design, but publishes only mean
absolute deviations, signed diagonal differences and scatterplots, no cell-level shares.

Partial exception worth considering: Figures 1 and 3 plot obtained against predicted for
every cell of every 4x4 and 2x2. Digitizing a scatterplot recovers the pairs
(CRR-predicted, obtained) but not which cell is which, so it would support a distributional
comparison of residuals against a Gaussian-race prediction, not a cell-by-cell one. Low
priority, but not worthless if the printed conclusion is the thing we want to overturn.

## What the authors concluded, quoted verbatim where possible

Hodge's summary in the abstract:

> "The results demonstrated: (a) The CRR predicts equally well the response proportions of
> single dimensional visual, kinesthetic, and auditory stimulus ensembles, but less well than
> those for multidimensional auditory stimuli. (b) Better predictions are obtained with four
> than with two stimulus objects. (c) The CRR is sensitive to variations in the spacing and
> range of the stimulus ensembles and to practice on the task. It is concluded that the rule
> tends to fail whenever stimulus conditions elicit differential amounts of stimulus and
> response confusion."

And the closing paragraph:

> "In conclusion, the present experiments demonstrate that: (a) CRR predictions are somewhat
> better for multidimensional than for unidimensional stimuli because of the absence of large
> differential amounts of stimulus and response confusion in multidimensional situations;
> (b) the CRR is sensitive to the effects of practice and stimulus spacing-range variables;
> (c) there is little difference in CRR predictions for auditory, visual, and kinesthetic
> stimulus dimensions; and (d) the operation of response biases make testing of the CRR with
> single dimensional stimuli very difficult. More satisfactory tests of the CRR will be
> possible only when response biases can either be controlled experimentally or their
> influence negated theoretically by adding a bias parameter to the model."

That last sentence is the opening this project walks through: Hodge diagnosed the residual as
a missing bias parameter within the ratio-rule framework and stopped. He also notes Clarke's
own view that the rule fails for unidimensional stimuli "because the ordering inherent in
single dimensional stimuli produces contextual constraints or biases which, by definition,
are incompatible with the CRR" (attributed to Clarke by personal communication, footnote 2).
