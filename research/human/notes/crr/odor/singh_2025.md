# Singh et al. 2025 — ScentAware digital odor ID, per-descriptor percentages printed (with "no odor")

## Citation
Singh J, Moustafa SA, Mondal S, et al. "Digital adaptation of odor identification test: Current
knowledge, gaps, and initial insights from a DAC global cohort." *Journal of Alzheimer's Disease
Reports* 2025;9:25424823251362132. doi:10.1177/25424823251362132. PMC12365445.

## Domain and stimuli
Olfaction, digital/self-administered cued identification. **ScentAware** test: 16 odorants (Banana,
Clove, Cinnamon, Orange, Lemon, Peppermint, Pineapple, Leather, Lavender, Rose, Smoke, Wood, Garlic,
Eucalyptus, Coffee, plus a repeated Peppermint for reliability). QR-coded inhaler tubes scanned with a
mobile device. Davos Alzheimer's Collaborative (DAC) global cohort; the printed table is the Egyptian
cohort, n = 125. Mean completion 7 minutes, ~25 s per item.

## Master response set and restricted response sets (nested, overlapping, or a relabelling)
**Fixed 5-option structure — 4 named descriptors plus an explicit "No Odor Detected" — applied
uniformly to every item.** Not nested and not varied.

Recorded here because the fifth option is structurally interesting: it is a designed *augmentation* of
the standard 4-AFC menu with an opt-out. Comparing these shares against a pure 4-AFC test on the same
odors would be an S ⊃ T comparison (4 descriptors ⊂ 4 descriptors + opt-out) — but the ScentAware
distractor words differ from the Sniffin' Sticks ones for the overlapping odors, so no clean pairing
exists with any dataset in this directory. The authors also note the opt-out is used for two different
reasons, which muddies its interpretation (quoted below).

## What numbers are printed or deposited (which tables/files, counts or proportions, per subject or pooled)
**Pooled per-descriptor percentages, printed in full — one of only two such tables found.**
Table 3: rows = the 16 odorant items, columns = the five response options (four named descriptors plus
"No Odor Detected"), cells = response frequency as a percentage, n = 125. Verified examples:
- Garlic: garlic 76.61, onion 9.68, carrot 3.23, vinegar 4.03, no-odor 6.45
- Rose: cookie 2.42, alcohol 4.84, rose 70.97, chamomile 12.10, no-odor 9.68

Per-subject data is **not** printed. Underlying records are promised via a gated platform:
> "Data will be made available through the Alzheimer's Drug Discovery Initiative (ADDI) Workbench."
No URL given in the statement.

## Usability verdict (usable now / needs digitizing / needs library access / unusable, and why)
**Needs digitizing — and it is cheap to do.** Table 3 is 16 rows × 5 columns of percentages in an
open-access HTML article; transcribe it and you have complete pooled choice shares over a 5-option set
for 16 odors. Small n (125) and pooled only, so no within-subject work is possible, but it is one of
the few places a *complete* option distribution is printed rather than summarized.

Apply to ADDI only if per-subject data is needed; the marginal value over the printed table is
uncertain and the cohort is small.

## What the authors concluded, quoted verbatim where possible
On the opt-out option, verbatim:
> "participants selected the 'No Odor Detected' option not only when unable to perceive an odor, but
> also when they could detect a scent but were unable to identify it"

That is a direct, authorial statement that an added alternative absorbed probability mass for two
distinct reasons — which is exactly the kind of contamination a menu-effect analysis has to model. The
paper's broader conclusions concern feasibility of digital olfactory testing across global cohorts and
the need for cultural adaptation of descriptors; intra-rater agreement on the duplicated peppermint
item was 75%.
