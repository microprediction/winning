\begin{abstract}
\noindent Thompson sampling plays each alternative with the posterior
probability that it is best, and entropy search chooses evaluations
by the same vector. Where the numbers themselves are needed they are
approximated: from marginal means and variances in bandits, and by
expectation propagation, at a cost quartic in the number of
candidates, in Bayesian optimization. We compute the vector by
quadrature, for all $n$ alternatives at once, in time linear in $n$
at fixed resolution, whenever the posterior covariance is a low-rank
factor term plus a diagonal. Simulation under common random numbers
and a Gaussian bandit with a factor prior both produce such
posteriors exactly. With the computed vector, a stopping rule stops
sooner than the marginal rules at the same nominal threshold,
sampling from it matches Thompson sampling's regret, and entropy
search can run on thousands of candidates rather than fifty.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

An analyst has twenty candidate inventory policies and estimates each
one's expected profit by simulation. A website has fifty
advertisements and records clicks on whichever one it shows. A chemist
has predicted binding strengths for a thousand candidate molecules. In
each case there are $n$ alternatives, and alternative $i$ has an
unknown value $\theta_i$: the policy's true expected profit, the
advertisement's true click rate, the molecule's true binding strength.
The data $\mathcal{D}$ collected so far give a posterior belief about
$\theta = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_n)$.

The posterior probability of optimality is the vector
\begin{equation}
p_i \;=\; \Pr\!\left(\theta_i = \max_j \theta_j \,\middle|\,
\mathcal{D}\right), \qquad i = 1, \dots, n.
\label{eq:pom}
\end{equation}
Its entry $p_i$ is the probability, given the data, that alternative
$i$ is truly the best. If $p_3 = 0.9$ for the inventory policies, there
is a ninety percent chance that policy~3 has the highest expected
profit.

Decisions about where to collect data use this vector. The analyst can
stop simulating once the largest $p_i$ exceeds $0.95$, and until then
can spend runs on the policies whose $p_i$ are close. The website can
show advertisement $i$ with probability $p_i$, which is Thompson
sampling. A rule that must respect constraints, or must be
reproducible without randomness, needs the numbers $p_i$ themselves.

When the beliefs about different alternatives are independent, each
$p_i$ is a one-dimensional integral. In practice they are usually
correlated, because alternatives share causes. Inventory policies
simulated on the same demand history all do well on a quiet day and
badly on a busy one. Advertisements aimed at the same audience have
similar click rates. Molecules built on the same scaffold bind
similarly. With correlated beliefs, $p_i$ is the probability that
the $n - 1$ differences $\theta_i - \theta_j$ are all positive, a
Gaussian orthant probability in $n - 1$ dimensions, and no general
fast method computes it.

Three literatures need this vector and approximate it. The first is
ranking and selection, which chooses the best of $n$ simulated
systems. Its procedures bound the probability of a correct selection
with Bonferroni-type inequalities, or estimate it by posterior
sampling. The joint vector is not evaluated.

The second is Bayesian bandits and reinforcement learning.
\citet{tarbouriech2023} call Equation~\eqref{eq:pom} the key object of
that field and write that computing it ``involves computing several
complicated integrals with respect to the posterior and is intractable
in most cases.'' Their method, VAPOR, replaces it with a variational
program. \citet{menet2026} fine-tune a large language model toward a policy
that uses only the marginal means and standard deviations of the
posterior.

The third is Bayesian optimization. Entropy search
\citep{hennig2012} takes the vector itself, the posterior probability
that the optimum lies at each of $n$ candidate points, as what an
optimizer should learn, and chooses each evaluation to reduce its
entropy. \citet{hennig2012} call the vector intractable, approximate
it by expectation propagation at a cost of $O(n^4)$, and run with
fifty candidates. Expectation propagation accepts any covariance.
The computation here accepts covariances whose dependence runs
through a few shared variables, described as factors, blocks, nested
groups or a tree, and it fits a dense covariance to that grammar on
intake, with the residual of the fit reported as its error. For such
covariances it is linear in $n$.

Correlation that comes from a few shared causes has a simple form.
Each value is its own part plus a weighted sum of $\rho$ shared
variables:
\begin{equation}
\theta_i \;=\; m_i + w_i' z + \varepsilon_i, \qquad
z \sim N(0, I_\rho), \quad \varepsilon_i \sim N(0, d_i),
\label{eq:factormodel}
\end{equation}
with all the $\varepsilon_i$ independent of each other and of $z$. For
the inventory policies, $z$ might be the overall demand level and its
volatility. The row $w_i$ says how strongly policy $i$ responds to each.
The covariance of $\theta$ is then
\begin{equation}
\Sigma \;=\; W W' + \mathrm{diag}(d), \qquad W \in
\mathbb{R}^{n \times \rho}, \quad \rho \ll n,
\label{eq:factor}
\end{equation}
where $W$ has rows $w_i'$. The variables in $z$ are called factors,
and $W$ holds the loadings.

For beliefs of this form, the whole vector is one integral over the
factors, and quadrature evaluates it for all $n$ alternatives at once
in time linear in $n$ at fixed resolution. The computation is the pricing pass for correlated contests in
\citet{cotton2026}, restated rather than derived. One standard model from each of the first two literatures produces a
posterior of exactly this form after every update: simulation under
common random numbers, and a Gaussian bandit with a factor prior. In Bayesian optimization the form is a modelling choice, a kernel
with finitely many features, or the intake fit of a dense kernel. In the simulation, a
stopping rule on the computed vector stops sooner than rules on the
marginal approximations at the same nominal threshold. In the bandit,
sampling from it incurs less regret than they do.

\section{The computation, restated from Cotton (2026)}
\label{sec:computation}

The computation is the correlated-contest pricing pass of
\citet{cotton2026}, restated in the notation of
Equation~\eqref{eq:factormodel} and specialised to Gaussian noise.
Nothing in it is new. The original is more general: it accepts any
base distribution, and a covariance given as factor loadings, as
blocks, as nested groups or as a tree, or a dense covariance fitted to
one of those structures on the way in, and it also inverts the
vector, recovering means from probabilities. The factor form of
Equation~\eqref{eq:factor} with Gaussian noise is the case needed
here.

Write the posterior in the form of Equation~\eqref{eq:factormodel},
$\theta = m + W z + \varepsilon$. Assume every $d_i > 0$, so the
maximum is almost surely unique and the $p_i$ sum to one.

The shared factors $z$ make the alternatives dependent. Given $z$,
nothing is shared, and the alternatives are independent with means
$m_i + w_i' z$ and variances $d_i$. The probability that $i$ is
best given $z$ is then a one-dimensional integral, and $p_i$ is its
average over $z$.

Let $F_{i|z}$ and $f_{i|z}$ be the conditional distribution and
density functions of $\theta_i$ given $z$. Alternative $i$ is the
maximum when every other alternative falls below it, so
\begin{equation}
p_i \;=\; \int_{\mathbb{R}^\rho} \phi_\rho(z) \int_{\mathbb{R}}
f_{i|z}(x) \prod_{j \neq i} F_{j|z}(x)\, dx \, dz .
\label{eq:integral}
\end{equation}

The inner integrands for different $i$ share the product
$G_z(x) = \prod_j F_{j|z}(x)$. Dividing out the $i$th term gives
every $p_i$ from the same product:
\begin{equation}
p_i \;=\; \mathbb{E}_z \int f_{i|z}(x)\,
\frac{G_z(x)}{F_{i|z}(x)} \, dx .
\label{eq:cavity}
\end{equation}
On a lattice of $L$ points in $x$, with $Q$ quadrature nodes in $z$,
the whole vector costs $O(nLQ)$. The accuracy depends on $L$, on the
lattice window and on $Q$, and the $Q$ needed grows with the number of
factors and the geometry of $W$.

Only differences between loadings matter. Let $P = I - \mathbf{1}
\mathbf{1}'/n$ and write $W z = (PW) z + \mathbf{1}(\bar{w}' z)$,
where $\bar{w}$ is the average row of $W$. The second term shifts
every alternative by the same amount and changes no ranking, so
$p(m, W, d) = p(m, PW, d)$. The number of factor dimensions the
integral has to resolve is the rank of $PW$, which centering can
reduce below the rank of $W$. Centering, and any orthogonal rotation
of the columns of $W$, leave the computed vector unchanged to
$10^{-11}$ or better, which is a check on an implementation. In the
aligned regime of the stopping experiment every row of $W$ is the
same, so $PW = 0$ and the contest is independent.

The integral is exact. Its evaluation is not, because the lattice in
$x$ and the quadrature rule over $z$ have finite resolution. The
lattice is converged, and the factor rule is accurate unless
correlations are extreme. For three alternatives with $m = 0$,
$W = (0, 1, 3)'$ and $d_i = 0.1$, two of the three correlations
exceed $0.9$, the exact vector is known in closed form from the
bivariate quadrant identity, and the default rule is off by $0.0015$
in total variation. With $2^{15}$ scrambled-Sobol nodes in place of
the default rule that error is gone. The experiments' correlations
are at most $0.75$, and on their posteriors the default rule's error
is about $10^{-6}$ per entry.\footnote{Measured on ten posteriors,
two from each regime of each experiment. Raising the lattice from
$257$ to $2{,}049$ points changes no entry by more than
$4 \times 10^{-16}$. Replacing the default factor rule by $2^{15}$
scrambled-Sobol nodes changes no entry by more than
$1.2 \times 10^{-6}$. Four million Monte Carlo draws differ from the
computed vectors by $1$ to $4 \times 10^{-4}$ per entry, their own
standard error, and the $2 \times 10^5$ draws taken at sixteen
posteriors during the experiments differ by at most $0.0042$ in total
variation, again their own noise. These are measurements, not error
bounds. The script is \texttt{research/rs\_crn/exp3\_quadrature}.}

\section{Two models whose posteriors have exact factor form}
\label{sec:posteriors}

The computation of Section~\ref{sec:computation} needs the posterior
covariance in the form of Equation~\eqref{eq:factor}. Two standard models produce it exactly after every update, one from
ranking and selection and one from bandits. In simulation, the shared factors come from running every
system on the same random inputs. In the bandit, they come from a
prior in which arms share features.

\subsection{Simulation with common random numbers}
\label{sec:crn}

The result of this subsection is simple to state. When $n$ systems are
simulated on shared random inputs, the uncertainty about their $n$
unknown means is correlated, and the derivation shows that this
correlation has the same shape as the shared inputs: a few common
factors plus independent noise, with the number of factors equal to
the number of shared input streams. Accumulating replicates sharpens
the posterior but never changes that shape. So the vector of
Section~\ref{sec:computation} can be computed after every replicate
in time linear in $n$, and the $n \times n$ posterior covariance is
never formed. The rest of the subsection sets up the model and proves
the claim.

A simulation study compares $n$ systems, such as the inventory
policies of the introduction, by running each one many times and
averaging its output. Each run is called a replicate. The unknown
$\theta_i$ is system $i$'s long-run average output.

A standard way to compare systems more precisely is to give every
system the same random inputs in each replicate. This is called
common random numbers. In replicate $r$, all twenty inventory policies
face the same simulated demand history. A high-demand history raises
every policy's profit, so the outputs move together, and the
differences between policies are measured more precisely than their
levels.

Summarise the shared random inputs of replicate $r$ by $\rho$
variables $F_r$, such as the demand level and its volatility. The
analyst does not record $F_r$. System $i$ responds to it with
loadings $v_i$, which are known, and has its own noise
$\varepsilon_{ir}$:
\begin{equation}
Y_{ir} \;=\; \theta_i + v_i' F_r + \varepsilon_{ir}, \qquad
F_r \sim N(0, I_\rho), \quad \varepsilon_{ir} \sim N(0, \sigma_i^2).
\end{equation}
The $F_r$ and $\varepsilon_{ir}$ are independent of each other and
across replicates. The loadings $v_i$ and noise scales $\sigma_i > 0$
are known properties of the simulation, and $F_r$ is latent. If
$F_r$ were recorded too, subtracting $v_i' F_r$ from each output would
remove the correlation and leave a diagonal posterior. The unknown is
$\theta$, with prior $\theta \sim N(0, s_0^2 I)$ and $s_0 > 0$.

The posterior covariance of $\theta$ has factor form, with the same
number of factors $\rho$. The derivation takes four
steps.

The covariance of one replicate across systems is
\begin{equation}
\Lambda \;=\; V V' + D, \qquad D = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1^2, \dots,
\sigma_n^2).
\end{equation}
After $n_r \ge 1$ replicates with mean vector $\bar{Y}$, the posterior
of $\theta$ is Gaussian with precision
\begin{equation}
A \;=\; s_0^{-2} I + n_r \Lambda^{-1}.
\label{eq:precision}
\end{equation}

The first step inverts $\Lambda$ with the Woodbury identity:
\begin{align}
\Lambda^{-1} &= D^{-1} - D^{-1} V M^{-1} V' D^{-1},
& M &= I_\rho + V' D^{-1} V.
\label{eq:wood1}
\end{align}
With $U = D^{-1} V$ and $S = n_r M^{-1}$, the precision becomes
\begin{align}
A &= \mathrm{diag}(a) - U S U', & a_i &= s_0^{-2} + n_r \sigma_i^{-2}.
\label{eq:diagminus}
\end{align}
This is a positive diagonal minus a positive semidefinite term of
rank at most $\rho$.

The second step inverts $A$, again with the Woodbury identity:
\begin{align}
A^{-1} &= \mathrm{diag}(a)^{-1} + \mathrm{diag}(a)^{-1} U \, C \, U'
\mathrm{diag}(a)^{-1},
& C &= \bigl(S^{-1} - U' \mathrm{diag}(a)^{-1} U\bigr)^{-1}.
\label{eq:wood2}
\end{align}

The third step shows that $C$ is positive definite, so that it has a
square root. Let
$K = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} \mathrm{diag}(a) & U \\ U' & S^{-1}
\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$. The Schur complement of $S^{-1}$ in $K$ is
$\mathrm{diag}(a) - USU' = A$, which is positive definite, and
$S^{-1}$ is positive definite, so $K$ is positive definite. The Schur
complement of $\mathrm{diag}(a)$ in $K$ is $C^{-1}$, so $C^{-1}$ is
positive definite too.

The fourth step gives the factor form:
\begin{equation}
\Sigma_{\text{post}} \;=\; \mathrm{diag}(d) + W W', \qquad
d_i = a_i^{-1}, \quad
W = \mathrm{diag}(a)^{-1} U \, C^{1/2},
\label{eq:postform}
\end{equation}
exactly, for every $n_r \ge 1$. Before any replicate the posterior is
the diagonal prior. The posterior mean is $m = A^{-1} h$ with
$h = n_r \Lambda^{-1} \bar{Y}$, and both factors are available from
Equations~\eqref{eq:wood1} and~\eqref{eq:wood2}.

No step approximates. The posterior has factor form because the shared
random inputs have factor form. The number of posterior factors is at
most $\rho$, the number of shared input variables, however many
systems are compared.

\subsection{A bandit whose arms share features}
\label{sec:bandit-posterior}

The result here is the same as for the simulation. When the prior on
the arms' mean rewards has a few common factors plus independent
noise, the posterior keeps that shape after every pull, with no more
factors than the prior, whatever arms are pulled and however often.

In a bandit, a decision maker repeatedly picks one of $n$ arms and
observes a noisy reward from it. Here $\theta_i$ is arm $i$'s mean
reward, such as an advertisement's click rate. Pulling arm $a$ returns
$\theta_a + N(0, \sigma_n^2)$, with $\sigma_n > 0$.

The correlation comes from the prior. Advertisements that share an
audience or a design are expected to have similar click rates. A
linear model expresses this: arm $i$ has known features $v_i$, and
$\theta_i = v_i' \beta + e_i$, with a shared $\beta \sim N(0,
I_\rho)$ and independent $e_i \sim N(0, d_{0i})$, $d_{0i} > 0$. The
prior is then $\theta \sim N(0, \Sigma_0)$ with
$\Sigma_0 = V V' + \mathrm{diag}(d_0)$, which has factor form.

A Gaussian-process prior with a kernel of finitely many features and
an independent residual has this form exactly. A dense kernel does
not, and an inducing-point approximation to one has a dense residual
\citep{titsias2009}, so for those a diagonal residual is an added
approximation.

With per-arm pull counts $c$, the posterior precision is
\begin{equation}
A \;=\; \Sigma_0^{-1} + \sigma_n^{-2}\,\mathrm{diag}(c).
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:wood1} applied to $\Sigma_0$ puts $A$ in the form
of Equation~\eqref{eq:diagminus}, with $a_i = d_{0i}^{-1} + c_i
\sigma_n^{-2}$, $S = M_0^{-1}$ and $M_0 = I_\rho + V'
\mathrm{diag}(d_0)^{-1} V$. The remaining steps of
Section~\ref{sec:crn} apply unchanged. The posterior is exactly
factor plus diagonal after every pull, with no more factors than the
prior.

The same posterior has a form with no subtraction inside an inverse.
Let $\lambda_i = c_i / \sigma_n^2$ be the observation precision
accumulated on arm $i$, $r_i = 1/(1 + d_{0i} \lambda_i)$,
$R = \mathrm{diag}(r)$ and $H = I_\rho + V' \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_i
r_i) V$. Then
\begin{equation}
\Sigma_{\text{post}} \;=\; \mathrm{diag}(d_{0i} r_i) + R V H^{-1} V' R,
\qquad W_{\text{post}} = R V H^{-1/2}.
\label{eq:stable}
\end{equation}
$H$ is the identity plus a positive semidefinite matrix, so it is
positive definite. An arm never pulled has $\lambda_i = 0$, and arms
observed with different noise levels enter through their own
$\lambda_i$. Equation~\eqref{eq:stable} agrees with direct inversion
of the dense precision to machine precision, and is the form to
implement.

\section{Thompson sampling and the approximations of the vector}
\label{sec:surrogates}

Bandit methods relate to the vector in two ways. Thompson sampling
uses it implicitly, without computing it. Two other methods compute approximations of it from
marginal means and variances. The experiments compare all three with
the computed vector.

\subsection{Thompson sampling selects arm $i$ with probability $p_i$}

Thompson sampling draws one sample $\tilde{\theta}$ from the
posterior and pulls the arm with the largest sampled value. The
probability that it pulls arm $i$ is the probability that arm $i$ is
largest under the posterior, which is $p_i$. Lemma~8 of
\citet{tarbouriech2023} states the general version: the expected
occupancy of Thompson sampling equals the posterior probability of
optimality.

Pulling an arm with probability $p_i$, called \emph{exact matching},
is therefore Thompson sampling in distribution. Pulling from the
computed vector $\hat p$ is Thompson sampling up to the quadrature
error, which is about $10^{-6}$ per entry on the bandit's posteriors.
The bandit experiment compares the two. The policy remains
randomized. The deterministic part is the evaluation of its
probabilities.

The computed vector gives the numbers themselves, which a single draw
does not. A stopping rule needs $\max_i p_i$, an allocator needs to
know which $p_i$ are close, and a reproducible policy needs the
probabilities it would otherwise sample.

\subsection{Approximations from marginal means and variances}

When the numbers $p_i$ are needed, current methods approximate them
using only each alternative's marginal mean $m_i$ and marginal
standard deviation $s_i = \sqrt{d_i + \|w_i\|^2}$. Both ignore the
correlation between alternatives.

\emph{F-LITE} \citep{menet2025} treats the alternatives as
independent, $\prod_i N(m_i, s_i^2)$, and approximates the
probability of being best by $\Phi((m_i - \kappa^*)/s_i)$, with a
threshold $\kappa^*$ chosen so that these sum to one. The threshold
step is a second approximation. With two independent arms, $N(1,1)$
and $N(0,1)$, it gives $0.691$ for the first arm, against the exact
$0.760$. F-LITE therefore departs from $p$ even when the posterior is
independent.

Discarding correlation can produce a much larger error, because the
marginals do not determine the vector, even within the covariance
class of Equation~\eqref{eq:factor}. Let $\theta_1 = E_1$ and
$\theta_i = \sqrt{1 - \epsilon}\, Z + \sqrt{\epsilon}\, E_i$ for
$i = 2, \dots, n$, with $Z$ and the $E_i$ independent standard
Gaussians. Every alternative has the same $N(0, 1)$ marginal, the
covariance is rank one plus a positive diagonal, and every correlation
is nonnegative. As $\epsilon \to 0$ the last $n - 1$ alternatives
become copies of one another, so $p_1 \to 1/2$ and every other $p_i
\to 1/(2(n-1))$. A method that uses only the marginals returns $1/n$
for every alternative, since the marginals are identical. With
$n = 100$ and $\epsilon = 10^{-4}$, $p_1 = 0.493$ against $0.01$, and
the total-variation error of the marginal answer tends to
$1/2 - 1/n$.

A second example fixes the mean gap and varies the size of a shared
shock. Let $\theta_1 = 3 + Z + \varepsilon_1$ and
$\theta_2 = Z + \varepsilon_2$, with $Z \sim N(0, \tau^2)$ and
independent $\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, 1)$. The shared $Z$ cancels from
the difference, so $p_1 = \Phi(3/\sqrt{2}) \approx 0.983$ for every
$\tau$. F-LITE sees only the marginal variances $1 + \tau^2$ and gives
$\Phi\bigl(3 / (2\sqrt{1 + \tau^2})\bigr)$, which is $0.933$ at
$\tau = 0$, $0.682$ at $\tau = 3$, and tends to one half as $\tau$
grows. The aligned regime of the stopping experiment is this example
with a hundred systems.

The shared product in Equation~\eqref{eq:integral} has a precedent in
the independent case. \citet{menet2025} write the independent
probability of being best as the same product integral, their
Equation~(2), and their Proposition~1 evaluates it by quadrature on
shared integration points with a convergence guarantee.
Equation~\eqref{eq:cavity} is that evaluator conditional on the
factors, averaged over them. Their A-LITE and F-LITE are
approximations of the independent integral: A-LITE fits a Gaussian to
the distribution of the largest competitor by matching quartiles, and
F-LITE replaces it by the shared threshold above. The experiments
include F-LITE only.

The \emph{VBOS policy} is the variational Bayesian optimistic sampling
rule of \citet{odonoghue2021}, extended by \citet{tarbouriech2023}. In
the near-closed form of \citet{menet2026} it is
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\pi}_i \;=\; v\!\left(\frac{m_i - \kappa^*}{s_i}\right),
\qquad v(c) = \exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{8}\bigl(\sqrt{c^2 + 4} -
c\bigr)^2\Bigr),
\label{eq:vbos}
\end{equation}
with $\kappa^*$ again chosen so the vector sums to one.

The VBOS policy departs from $p$ on purpose, and its regret analysis
depends on that departure. It is a policy, not an estimate of $p$.
Its distance from $p$ matters because \citet{menet2026} use it as
the trainable substitute for $p$.

\section{First experiment: when a simulation study can stop}
\label{sec:stopping}

The first experiment runs the simulation study of
Section~\ref{sec:crn}, in which $n$ systems are simulated on shared
random inputs, and asks when it can stop. A simulation study stops
when it is confident enough that it has found the best system. The
experiment asks whether a stopping rule
that uses the computed vector stops earlier than rules that use the
marginal approximations, and whether it still selects the best system
as often as its confidence level promises. Correlation is the only
thing that separates the rules, so everything else is held fixed and
only the correlation geometry varies.

The setting is the posterior of Section~\ref{sec:crn} with $n = 100$
systems, $\rho = 2$ scenario streams and prior scale $s_0 = 1$. True
means are drawn from the prior. Every system has replicate variance
one, so each replicate carries the same marginal information in every
regime, and a marginal-only rule faces the same problem in every
regime. The \emph{factor share} is the fraction of that variance that
comes from the shared scenario.

Correlation can mislead a marginal-only rule in two directions. If
the leading systems move together, their difference is less uncertain
than their marginals suggest, and the rule is too cautious. If they
move oppositely, their difference is more uncertain, and the rule is
too confident. The regimes produce each case, with an uncorrelated
control.

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Independent}: factor share zero. This is the control.
\item \emph{Aligned}: every system loads on the same stream, with
factor share $0.6$. Differences between systems then vary less than
their marginal variances suggest.
\item \emph{Opposed}: half the systems load positively and half
negatively on one stream, with factor share $0.6$. Differences between
systems in opposite halves then vary more than their marginal
variances suggest.
\end{itemize}

The experiment compares three stopping rules, which differ only in
how they estimate $p$. The exact rule uses the computation of
Section~\ref{sec:computation}, and the other two use F-LITE and the
VBOS policy. After every replicate, a rule recomputes its estimate of $p$, and it
stops the first time the largest entry reaches $1 - \delta$, with
$\delta = 0.05$, selecting that entry.

A fourth rule is the one a practitioner would reach for: draw $M$
samples from the correlated posterior and estimate $p$ by the argmax
frequencies. It uses the correlation, costs $O(M n \rho)$, and needs
$M$ of about $500$ to resolve a threshold of $0.95$ to within $0.01$,
with the error growing as the threshold is approached. It is not run.
The comparators run are the two marginal rules from the literature,
and the comparison with the Monte Carlo rule at matched cost is
open.

For the true vector the rule has a Bayesian guarantee. Let $\tau$ be
the first time $\max_i p_i \ge 1 - \delta$, let $B$ be the budget, and
on $\{\tau \le B\}$ let $I$ be the selected alternative and $I^*$ the
best. Then
\begin{equation}
\Pr(I \ne I^* \mid \tau \le B)
\;=\; \mathbb{E}\bigl[1 - p_I(\mathcal{D}_\tau) \mid \tau \le B\bigr]
\;\le\; \delta .
\end{equation}
Optional stopping does not affect this statement, which averages over
the prior. It is not a frequentist guarantee for a fixed $\theta$, and
it applies to the true $p$. For the computed vector $\hat p$, if
$\|\hat p - p\|_\infty \le \eta$ at stopping, then stopping at
$\max_i \hat p_i \ge 1 - \delta$ gives error at most $\delta + \eta$,
and a threshold of $1 - \delta + \eta$ restores $\delta$. On the stopping
posteriors, $\eta$ is about $10^{-6}$ by the measurements of
Section~\ref{sec:computation}, which are measurements rather than a
bound.

The three rules share a nominal threshold, not a true confidence
level. With two independent arms of equal posterior standard
deviation $s$, an entry of $0.95$ requires a mean gap of $2.33\,s$ for
the exact vector, $3.29\,s$ for F-LITE and $4.84\,s$ for the VBOS
policy. The true posterior probability of a correct selection at
those gaps is $0.950$, $0.990$ and $0.9997$. Part of any extra
sampling by the marginal rules is this higher effective target.

\section{Second experiment: a bandit with correlated arms}
\label{sec:bandit}

The second experiment is different from the first. It runs the
bandit of Section~\ref{sec:bandit-posterior}, in which arms share
features through a factor prior, and compares the four policies of
Section~\ref{sec:surrogates}. It asks two things. The first is
whether pulling
arms with the computed probabilities reproduces the regret of Thompson
sampling, as Lemma~8 requires of the true vector. The second is how much regret the two marginal approximations lose
when their probabilities are used as the policy.

Thompson sampling here draws from the full correlated posterior, so it already uses
every correlation the computed vector uses. It is the benchmark the
computed vector has to match, not a baseline it is expected to beat.
The policies that lose are the two marginal approximations.

The setting is the posterior of Section~\ref{sec:bandit-posterior}
with $n = 50$ arms, prior variance one per arm, observation noise
$\sigma_n = 1$, horizon 200 and 100 replications. The prior's factor
term has two columns. In the \emph{independent} regime it is zero. In the
\emph{clusters} regime each half of the arms loads on its own factor,
which carries $0.75$ of the prior variance.

The experiment compares four policies on the same posterior:
Thompson sampling, exact matching, sampling from the VBOS policy, and
sampling from F-LITE. Table~\ref{tab:bandit} reports cumulative
Bayes regret and, at the horizon, whether the arm with the highest
posterior mean is the true best and the shortfall of that arm's value
below the best. Its last column is the total-variation distance
between each policy's action distribution and the exact vector at
mid-horizon, along that policy's own trajectory.

\section{Scope}
\label{sec:limits}

The derivations of Section~\ref{sec:posteriors} are exact because the
observations there are generated with factor structure. When scenario
effects enter a simulation nonlinearly, a low-rank model of $\Lambda$
is an approximation, and the posterior inherits its error. When a Gaussian-process kernel is dense with a short length scale,
the intake fit to the grammar has to precede the computation, and its
residual is the error.

The vector says how uncertain the choice of best is. It does not say
how much one more measurement would reduce that uncertainty, which is
what a budget allocator needs. That quantity is the knowledge
gradient \citep{frazier2008}, an expectation over what the posterior
means will become after the measurement, and \citet{tuisov2026}
treats it as the myopic value of computation. The computation here does not supply it.

Three one-step criteria
should be kept apart. Reducing the entropy of $p$ is entropy search.
Raising $\max_i p_i$ aims at correct identification under zero-one
loss, where choosing a near-identical runner-up counts as an error.
Both are objectives of an optimizer rather than of a bandit, a
distinction \citet{hennig2012} draw: an optimizer infers the minimum
whatever the values at its evaluation points, and a bandit is scored
on those values.

The knowledge gradient raises the expected value of the final choice
and cares about the size of the differences. The vector supplies the
first two directly. An allocator that spends where the entries of $p$
are close is a heuristic for the second, not a criterion.

The regret analysis of \citet{odonoghue2021} depends on the VBOS
tilt. Its measured distance from $p$ quantifies the approximation, but
does not by itself make a case against the tilt.

Thompson sampling needs one posterior sample and no vector, and the
bandit experiment can be run that way. The computed vector matters
where a sample is not enough: stopping, allocation, constraints and
derandomization. The stopping problem is of that kind.

The stopping experiment's loadings have rank at most one, and in the
aligned regime the centered loadings are zero, so the exact rule faces
an independent contest there while the marginal rules do not know it.
Loadings that vary across both columns, with unequal residual
variances, would test the computation where the correlation does not
cancel.

\section{Discussion}

Thompson sampling runs in production wherever alternatives are
compared under uncertainty: advertising, recommendation, clinical
allocation, and now the fine-tuning of language models toward it
\citep{menet2026}. Its action probabilities are the vector $p$. The
policy never needs them as numbers, but much that surrounds the
policy does.

Off-policy evaluation needs them as propensities, and an audit of why
an arm was shown needs them. A stopping rule, a shortlist of the $k$
arms most likely to be best, a constraint on how often any arm may be
shown, and a reproducible version of the policy all need them. In
every one of these uses they have been approximated, from marginals
or by counting posterior draws.

The best-known Thompson samplers are in the class. Linear Thompson
sampling \citep{agrawal2013}, the version \citet{chapelle2011}
evaluated on display advertising and news recommendation, puts a
Gaussian posterior on a coefficient vector $\beta$ of dimension equal
to the number of features, and the arm means $\theta_i = x_i' \beta$
then have covariance $X \Sigma_\beta X'$. That is Equation~\eqref{eq:factor}
with the feature matrix as the loadings and a factor rank equal to
the number of features, and Section~\ref{sec:bandit-posterior} keeps
it in that form after every pull. The offline evaluation used for
those samplers, the replay method of \citet{li2011}, needs a
uniformly random log because the sampler's own propensities were not
available, and it discards every logged event whose random arm
differs from the policy's choice, about all but one in $K$ for $K$
arms. With the propensities computed, the production log itself becomes
the evaluation set.

Bayesian optimization has the same object. Entropy search
\citep{hennig2012} takes the posterior probability that the minimum
lies at each of $n$ representer points as the quantity an optimizer
should learn, and scores a belief by its relative entropy from a
uniform base measure. On the representer points that probability is
Equation~\eqref{eq:pom} in the minimum convention, a Gaussian
integral over a polyhedral cone, which \citet{hennig2012} call
intractable after \citet{plackett1954}. They approximate it by
expectation propagation at a cost of $O(n^4)$ per vector, which
limits $n$ to well below a thousand, and their implementation uses
fifty representer points and about ten seconds per choice of
evaluation point. Their alternative, sampling functions and recording
each minimum, costs $O(S n^3)$ for $S$ samples and has no
derivatives.

For a factor-structured belief the computation here gives the same
vector in time linear in $n$, so thousands of representer points cost
milliseconds. The price is the covariance class. Expectation propagation takes any
dense covariance. A dense kernel on the representer points has to be
fitted to the grammar of factors, blocks, nested groups and trees
before the computation here applies. That fit is the dense-covariance
intake of \citet{cotton2026}, and its residual is the error.
Entropy search also uses the derivatives of the vector with respect
to the mean and covariance for a first-order expansion of the
expected information gain. The computation here supplies the exact
Jacobian with respect to the means, and not the covariance
derivatives.

The innovation formulas of \citet{hennig2012} give the hypothetical
posterior after a measurement for any Gaussian process, and the
closure of Section~\ref{sec:posteriors} is the case in which that
posterior stays in factor form. The entropy-search acquisition
$\alpha(a) = H(p) - \mathbb{E}_{Y_a \mid \mathcal{D}}[H(p^{Y_a})]$
then needs the closure, the computation, and a scalar quadrature over
the measurement outcome. The scope is finite candidates and factor
beliefs.

How much better the computed numbers are than counted draws depends
on the factor rank. On the posteriors of the two experiments, where
the centred loadings have rank at most two, one evaluation of a
hundred alternatives takes about ten milliseconds and is accurate to
$10^{-6}$ per entry. Counting draws for ten milliseconds gives about
twenty thousand draws, and an entry near $0.01$ is then known to
seven percent of itself. Matching $10^{-6}$ by counting would take
$10^{11}$ draws. At factor ranks three to five, on single
hundred-arm posteriors of a linear sampler, one evaluation takes
$0.3$ to $1$ second and $200{,}000$ draws take $0.3$ second, with
errors of the same order. The advantage over counting is a low-rank
advantage, and centring the loadings is what decides the rank.

Against the marginal shortcuts the comparison does not depend on
rank. Their error can approach one half with every marginal held
fixed, and in the stopping experiment they spent $1.5$ to $3.4$ times
the replicates at the same threshold. Thompson sampling itself is not
at fault, since it uses the correlation. The loss is confined to the
side that computes probabilities, and that is the side the computed
vector replaces.

Four things are open. The Monte Carlo rule on the correlated
posterior, the practitioner's alternative, has not been compared at
matched cost across ranks. Shortlisting, where $\sum_{i \in S} p_i$
is the exact chance that the winner is retained, is the application
where the whole vector should change a decision most visibly, and has
not been run. And the fine-tuning target of \citet{menet2026} uses
marginals because the true vector was out of reach. For a factor
posterior it no longer is. And entropy search on a large candidate
set with a few shared factors would show whether the computed vector
makes that acquisition cheaper or more accurate than expectation
propagation.
