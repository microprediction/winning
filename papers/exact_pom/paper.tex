\begin{abstract}
\noindent Thompson sampling plays each alternative with the posterior
probability that it is best. Methods that need that probability
itself approximate it from marginal means and variances. We compute it
directly, for all $n$ alternatives at once in time linear in $n$,
whenever the posterior covariance is a low-rank factor term plus a
diagonal. Simulation under common random numbers and a Gaussian
bandit with a factor prior both produce such posteriors exactly. In
both, the computed vector stops sooner than the marginal
approximations at the same nominal threshold, and incurs less regret.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

An analyst has twenty candidate inventory policies and estimates each
one's expected profit by simulation. A website has fifty
advertisements and records clicks on whichever one it shows. A chemist
has predicted binding strengths for a thousand candidate molecules. In
each case there are $n$ alternatives, and alternative $i$ has an
unknown value $\theta_i$: the policy's true expected profit, the
advertisement's true click rate, the molecule's true binding strength.
The data $\mathcal{D}$ collected so far give a posterior belief about
$\theta = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_n)$.

The posterior probability of optimality is the vector
\begin{equation}
p_i \;=\; \Pr\!\left(\theta_i = \max_j \theta_j \,\middle|\,
\mathcal{D}\right), \qquad i = 1, \dots, n.
\label{eq:pom}
\end{equation}
Its entry $p_i$ is the probability, given the data, that alternative
$i$ is truly the best. If $p_3 = 0.9$ for the inventory policies, there
is a ninety percent chance that policy~3 has the highest expected
profit.

Decisions about where to collect data use this vector. The analyst can
stop simulating once the largest $p_i$ exceeds $0.95$, and until then
can spend runs on the policies whose $p_i$ are close. The website can
show advertisement $i$ with probability $p_i$, which is Thompson
sampling. A rule that must respect constraints, or must be
reproducible without randomness, needs the numbers $p_i$ themselves.

When the beliefs about different alternatives are independent, each
$p_i$ is a one-dimensional integral. In practice they are usually
correlated, because alternatives share causes. Inventory policies
simulated on the same demand history all do well on a quiet day and
badly on a busy one. Advertisements aimed at the same audience have
similar click rates. Molecules built on the same scaffold bind
similarly. With correlated beliefs, $p_i$ is an $n$-dimensional
Gaussian orthant probability, and no general fast method computes it.

Two literatures need this vector and approximate it. The first is
ranking and selection, which chooses the best of $n$ simulated
systems. Its procedures bound the probability of a correct selection
with Bonferroni-type inequalities, or estimate it by posterior
sampling. The joint vector is not evaluated.

The second is Bayesian bandits and reinforcement learning.
\citet{tarbouriech2023} call Equation~\eqref{eq:pom} the key object of
that field and write that computing it ``involves computing several
complicated integrals with respect to the posterior and is intractable
in most cases.'' Their method, VAPOR, replaces it with a variational
program. \citet{menet2026} fine-tune a large language model toward a
policy that uses only the marginal means and standard deviations of
the posterior. Section~\ref{sec:surrogates} describes these
approximations.

Correlation that comes from a few shared causes has a simple form.
Each value is its own part plus a weighted sum of $\rho$ shared
variables:
\begin{equation}
\theta_i \;=\; m_i + w_i' z + \varepsilon_i, \qquad
z \sim N(0, I_\rho), \quad \varepsilon_i \sim N(0, d_i),
\label{eq:factormodel}
\end{equation}
with all the $\varepsilon_i$ independent of each other and of $z$. For
the inventory policies, $z$ might be the overall demand level and its
volatility. The row $w_i$ says how strongly policy $i$ responds to each.
The covariance of $\theta$ is then
\begin{equation}
\Sigma \;=\; W W' + \mathrm{diag}(d), \qquad W \in
\mathbb{R}^{n \times \rho}, \quad \rho \ll n,
\label{eq:factor}
\end{equation}
where $W$ has rows $w_i'$. The variables in $z$ are called factors,
and $W$ holds the loadings.

For beliefs of this form, Equation~\eqref{eq:pom} can be computed for
all $n$ alternatives at once, in time linear in $n$.
Section~\ref{sec:computation} gives the computation, which is the
pricing pass for correlated contests in \citet{cotton2026}.
Section~\ref{sec:posteriors} shows that one standard model from each
literature produces a posterior of exactly this form after every
update. Section~\ref{sec:surrogates} relates the vector to Thompson
sampling and to the approximations above.
Sections~\ref{sec:stopping} and~\ref{sec:bandit} measure what using
the vector changes, in a simulation stopping problem and in a bandit.
Section~\ref{sec:limits} states where the exact form fails.

\section{Computing the vector for beliefs with shared factors}
\label{sec:computation}

Write the posterior in the form of Equation~\eqref{eq:factormodel},
$\theta = m + W z + \varepsilon$. Assume every $d_i > 0$, so the
maximum is almost surely unique and the $p_i$ sum to one.

The shared factors $z$ are what make the alternatives dependent. Fix
$z$, and nothing is shared: the alternatives are independent, with
means $m_i + w_i' z$ and variances $d_i$. The probability that $i$ is
best given $z$ is then a one-dimensional integral, and $p_i$ is its
average over $z$.

Let $F_{i|z}$ and $f_{i|z}$ be the conditional distribution and
density functions of $\theta_i$ given $z$. Alternative $i$ is the
maximum when every other alternative falls below it, so
\begin{equation}
p_i \;=\; \int_{\mathbb{R}^\rho} \phi_\rho(z) \int_{\mathbb{R}}
f_{i|z}(x) \prod_{j \neq i} F_{j|z}(x)\, dx \, dz .
\label{eq:integral}
\end{equation}

The inner integrands for different $i$ share the product
$G_z(x) = \prod_j F_{j|z}(x)$. Dividing out the $i$th term gives
every $p_i$ from the same product:
\begin{equation}
p_i \;=\; \mathbb{E}_z \int f_{i|z}(x)\,
\frac{G_z(x)}{F_{i|z}(x)} \, dx .
\label{eq:cavity}
\end{equation}
On a lattice of $L$ points in $x$, with $Q$ quadrature nodes in $z$,
the whole vector costs $O(nLQ)$. The accuracy depends on $L$, on the
lattice window and on $Q$, and the $Q$ needed grows with the number of
factors and the geometry of $W$.

The integral is exact, but its evaluation is numerical. With three
alternatives, $W = (0, 1, 3)'$ and $d_i = 0.1$, the vector is known
analytically from the bivariate quadrant identity, and the default
settings of the implementation are in error by total variation
$0.0015$. Raising the factor quadrature resolution reduces this error.

The numerical details are in \citet{cotton2026}: lattice placement,
products taken in logs, and the tails. The computation there is more
general than this paper needs. It accepts any base distribution, not
only the Gaussian, and a covariance given as factor loadings, as
blocks, as nested groups or as a tree, or a dense covariance that is
fitted to one of those structures on the way in. It also inverts the
vector, recovering means from probabilities. The factor form of
Equation~\eqref{eq:factor} with Gaussian noise is the case used here.
That implementation computes the probability of being the minimum,
and the maximum is obtained by negating $m$ and $W$. It is used here
without other change. The
computed vector was compared with argmax frequencies from
$2 \times 10^5$ Monte Carlo draws at sixteen posteriors: up to four
per regime in the stopping experiment, at the twentieth replicate,
and two per regime in the bandit, at round 50. The largest
total-variation distance in those comparisons is $0.0042$, which is at
the Monte Carlo noise level for that sample size. These comparisons
are statistical checks, not error bounds.

\section{Two models whose posteriors have exact factor form}
\label{sec:posteriors}

The computation of Section~\ref{sec:computation} needs the posterior
covariance in the form of Equation~\eqref{eq:factor}. Two standard
models produce it exactly after every update, one from each
literature. In simulation, the shared factors come from running every
system on the same random inputs. In the bandit, they come from a
prior in which arms share features.

\subsection{Simulation with common random numbers}
\label{sec:crn}

A simulation study compares $n$ systems, such as the inventory
policies of the introduction, by running each one many times and
averaging its output. Each run is called a replicate. The unknown
$\theta_i$ is system $i$'s long-run average output.

A standard way to compare systems more precisely is to give every
system the same random inputs in each replicate. This is called
common random numbers. In replicate $r$, all twenty inventory policies
face the same simulated demand history. A high-demand history raises
every policy's profit, so the outputs move together, and the
differences between policies are measured more precisely than their
levels.

Summarise the shared random inputs of replicate $r$ by $\rho$
variables $F_r$, such as the demand level and its volatility. System
$i$ responds to them with loadings $v_i$ and has its own noise
$\varepsilon_{ir}$:
\begin{equation}
Y_{ir} \;=\; \theta_i + v_i' F_r + \varepsilon_{ir}, \qquad
F_r \sim N(0, I_\rho), \quad \varepsilon_{ir} \sim N(0, \sigma_i^2).
\end{equation}
The $F_r$ and $\varepsilon_{ir}$ are independent of each other and
across replicates. The loadings $v_i$ and noise scales $\sigma_i > 0$
are known properties of the simulation. The unknown is $\theta$, with
prior $\theta \sim N(0, s_0^2 I)$ and $s_0 > 0$.

The posterior covariance of $\theta$ turns out to have factor form,
with the same number of factors $\rho$. The derivation takes four
steps.

The covariance of one replicate across systems is
\begin{equation}
\Lambda \;=\; V V' + D, \qquad D = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1^2, \dots,
\sigma_n^2).
\end{equation}
After $n_r \ge 1$ replicates with mean vector $\bar{Y}$, the posterior
of $\theta$ is Gaussian with precision
\begin{equation}
A \;=\; s_0^{-2} I + n_r \Lambda^{-1}.
\label{eq:precision}
\end{equation}

The first step inverts $\Lambda$ with the Woodbury identity:
\begin{align}
\Lambda^{-1} &= D^{-1} - D^{-1} V M^{-1} V' D^{-1},
& M &= I_\rho + V' D^{-1} V.
\label{eq:wood1}
\end{align}
With $U = D^{-1} V$ and $S = n_r M^{-1}$, the precision becomes
\begin{align}
A &= \mathrm{diag}(a) - U S U', & a_i &= s_0^{-2} + n_r \sigma_i^{-2}.
\label{eq:diagminus}
\end{align}
This is a positive diagonal minus a positive semidefinite term of
rank at most $\rho$.

The second step inverts $A$, again with the Woodbury identity:
\begin{align}
A^{-1} &= \mathrm{diag}(a)^{-1} + \mathrm{diag}(a)^{-1} U \, C \, U'
\mathrm{diag}(a)^{-1},
& C &= \bigl(S^{-1} - U' \mathrm{diag}(a)^{-1} U\bigr)^{-1}.
\label{eq:wood2}
\end{align}

The third step shows that $C$ is positive definite, so that it has a
square root. Let
$K = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} \mathrm{diag}(a) & U \\ U' & S^{-1}
\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$. The Schur complement of $S^{-1}$ in $K$ is
$\mathrm{diag}(a) - USU' = A$, which is positive definite, and
$S^{-1}$ is positive definite, so $K$ is positive definite. The Schur
complement of $\mathrm{diag}(a)$ in $K$ is $C^{-1}$, so $C^{-1}$ is
positive definite too.

The fourth step reads off the factor form:
\begin{equation}
\Sigma_{\text{post}} \;=\; \mathrm{diag}(d) + W W', \qquad
d_i = a_i^{-1}, \quad
W = \mathrm{diag}(a)^{-1} U \, C^{1/2},
\label{eq:postform}
\end{equation}
exactly, for every $n_r \ge 1$. Before any replicate the posterior is
the diagonal prior. The posterior mean is $m = A^{-1} h$ with
$h = n_r \Lambda^{-1} \bar{Y}$, and both factors are available from
Equations~\eqref{eq:wood1} and~\eqref{eq:wood2}.

No step approximates. The posterior has factor form because the shared
random inputs have factor form. The number of posterior factors is at
most $\rho$, the number of shared input variables, however many
systems are compared.

\subsection{A bandit whose arms share features}
\label{sec:bandit-posterior}

In a bandit, a decision maker repeatedly picks one of $n$ arms and
observes a noisy reward from it. Here $\theta_i$ is arm $i$'s mean
reward, such as an advertisement's click rate. Pulling arm $a$ returns
$\theta_a + N(0, \sigma_n^2)$, with $\sigma_n > 0$.

The correlation comes from the prior. Advertisements that share an
audience or a design are expected to have similar click rates. A
linear model expresses this: arm $i$ has known features $v_i$, and
$\theta_i = v_i' \beta + e_i$, with a shared $\beta \sim N(0,
I_\rho)$ and independent $e_i \sim N(0, d_{0i})$, $d_{0i} > 0$. The
prior is then $\theta \sim N(0, \Sigma_0)$ with
$\Sigma_0 = V V' + \mathrm{diag}(d_0)$, which has factor form.

Gaussian-process priors have this form only when the approximation
replaces the conditional residual by a diagonal. A standard
inducing-point posterior has a dense residual \citep{titsias2009}, so
for those the diagonal is an additional approximation.

With per-arm pull counts $c$, the posterior precision is
\begin{equation}
A \;=\; \Sigma_0^{-1} + \sigma_n^{-2}\,\mathrm{diag}(c).
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:wood1} applied to $\Sigma_0$ puts $A$ in the form
of Equation~\eqref{eq:diagminus}, with $a_i = d_{0i}^{-1} + c_i
\sigma_n^{-2}$, $S = M_0^{-1}$ and $M_0 = I_\rho + V'
\mathrm{diag}(d_0)^{-1} V$. The remaining steps of
Section~\ref{sec:crn} apply unchanged. The posterior is exactly
factor plus diagonal after every pull, with no more factors than the
prior.

\section{Thompson sampling and the approximations of the vector}
\label{sec:surrogates}

Bandit methods relate to the vector in two ways. Thompson sampling
uses it implicitly, without computing it. Two other methods compute
approximations of it from marginal means and variances. The
experiments of Sections~\ref{sec:stopping} and~\ref{sec:bandit}
compare all of them with the computed vector.

\subsection{Thompson sampling plays the vector}

Thompson sampling draws one sample $\tilde{\theta}$ from the
posterior and pulls the arm with the largest sampled value. The
probability that it pulls arm $i$ is the probability that arm $i$ is
largest under the posterior, which is $p_i$. Lemma~8 of
\citet{tarbouriech2023} states the general version: the expected
occupancy of Thompson sampling equals the posterior probability of
optimality.

Pulling an arm with probability $p_i$ from the computed vector, called
\emph{exact matching} below, is therefore Thompson sampling in
distribution, up to the quadrature error of
Section~\ref{sec:computation}. The two have the same expected regret,
and Section~\ref{sec:bandit} compares them.

The computed vector gives what a single draw cannot: the numbers
themselves. A stopping rule needs $\max_i p_i$, an allocator needs to
know which $p_i$ are close, and a reproducible policy needs the
probabilities it would otherwise sample.

\subsection{Approximations from marginal means and variances}

When the numbers $p_i$ are needed, current methods approximate them
using only each alternative's marginal mean $m_i$ and marginal
standard deviation $s_i = \sqrt{d_i + \|w_i\|^2}$. Both ignore the
correlation between alternatives.

\emph{F-LITE} \citep{menet2025} treats the alternatives as
independent, $\prod_i N(m_i, s_i^2)$, and approximates the
probability of being best by $\Phi((m_i - \kappa^*)/s_i)$, with a
threshold $\kappa^*$ chosen so that these sum to one. The threshold
step is a second approximation. With two independent arms, $N(1,1)$
and $N(0,1)$, it gives $0.691$ for the first arm, against the exact
$0.760$. F-LITE therefore departs from $p$ even when the posterior is
independent.

The \emph{VBOS policy} is the variational Bayesian optimistic sampling
rule of \citet{odonoghue2021}, extended by \citet{tarbouriech2023}. In
the near-closed form of \citet{menet2026} it is
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\pi}_i \;=\; v\!\left(\frac{m_i - \kappa^*}{s_i}\right),
\qquad v(c) = \exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{8}\bigl(\sqrt{c^2 + 4} -
c\bigr)^2\Bigr),
\label{eq:vbos}
\end{equation}
with $\kappa^*$ again chosen so the vector sums to one.

The VBOS policy departs from $p$ on purpose, and its regret analysis
depends on that departure. It is a policy, not an estimate of $p$.
Its distance from $p$ is reported below because \citet{menet2026}
use it as the trainable substitute for $p$.

\section{Stopping under common random numbers}
\label{sec:stopping}

The experiment uses the posterior of Section~\ref{sec:crn} with
$n = 100$ systems, $\rho = 2$ scenario streams and prior scale
$s_0 = 1$. True means are drawn from the prior. Every system has
replicate variance one, so each replicate carries the same marginal
information in every regime. The \emph{factor share} is the fraction
of that variance that comes from the shared scenario.

The regimes differ only in how systems load on the scenario streams:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Independent}: factor share zero. This is the control.
\item \emph{Aligned}: every system loads on the same stream, with
factor share $0.6$. Differences between systems then vary less than
their marginal variances suggest.
\item \emph{Opposed}: half the systems load positively and half
negatively on one stream, with factor share $0.6$. Differences between
systems in opposite halves then vary more than their marginal
variances suggest.
\end{itemize}

Three rules compete. Each computes its own estimate of $p$ after
every replicate, stops the first time its largest entry reaches
$1 - \delta$ with $\delta = 0.05$, and selects that entry. The exact
rule uses the computation of Section~\ref{sec:computation}. The other
two use F-LITE and the VBOS policy.

For the true vector the rule has a Bayesian guarantee. Let $\tau$ be
the first time $\max_i p_i \ge 1 - \delta$, let $B$ be the budget, and
on $\{\tau \le B\}$ let $I$ be the selected alternative and $I^*$ the
best. Then
\begin{equation}
\Pr(I \ne I^* \mid \tau \le B)
\;=\; \mathbb{E}\bigl[1 - p_I(\mathcal{D}_\tau) \mid \tau \le B\bigr]
\;\le\; \delta .
\end{equation}
Optional stopping does not affect this statement, which averages over
the prior. It is not a frequentist guarantee for a fixed $\theta$, and
it applies to the true $p$, not to a numerical approximation of it.

The three rules share a nominal threshold, not a true confidence
level. With two independent arms of equal posterior standard
deviation $s$, an entry of $0.95$ requires a mean gap of $2.33\,s$ for
the exact vector, $3.29\,s$ for F-LITE and $4.84\,s$ for the VBOS
policy. The true posterior probability of a correct selection at
those gaps is $0.950$, $0.990$ and $0.9997$. Part of any extra
sampling by the marginal rules is this higher effective target.

In Table~\ref{tab:stopping}, every rule's realized PCS among its
stopped runs is at or above nominal, within binomial noise. The exact
rule's $0.944$ in the opposed regime has 179 stopped runs and a
standard error of $0.016$.

At this common nominal threshold and budget, the two marginal-only
rules used $1.5$ to $3.4$ times the capped mean replicates of the
exact rule, and hit the budget far more often. The gap is largest in
the aligned regime, where the leading systems' differences are
tightly coupled. There the exact rule stops after $69.7$ replicates on
average, against $175.6$ and $240.1$, and stops in 191 of 200 runs,
against 140 and 109. An efficiency comparison at a matched true error
would need thresholds calibrated to a common PCS, which this
experiment does not do.

The marginal-only estimates are far from the exact vector along the
way. Before stopping in the aligned regime, F-LITE is at median
total-variation distance $0.123$ from the
exact vector, with ninetieth percentile $0.174$. The VBOS policy is at
$0.141$, with ninetieth percentile $0.206$.

The distances do not vanish in the independent regime, where they are
$0.063$ and $0.096$. F-LITE's threshold step is biased even when the
posterior is independent, and the VBOS policy departs from $p$ by
design. At the stopping experiment's Monte Carlo checks, the largest
total-variation distance of the computed vector was $0.0015$.

\section{A correlated Gaussian bandit}
\label{sec:bandit}

The bandit uses the posterior of Section~\ref{sec:bandit-posterior}
with $n = 50$ arms, prior variance one per arm, observation noise
$\sigma_n = 1$, horizon 200 and 100 replications. The prior's factor
term has two columns. In the \emph{independent} regime it is zero. In the
\emph{clusters} regime each half of the arms loads on its own factor,
which carries $0.75$ of the prior variance.

Four policies act on the same posterior: Thompson sampling, exact
matching, sampling from the VBOS policy, and sampling from F-LITE. Table~\ref{tab:bandit} reports cumulative
Bayes regret and, at the horizon, whether the arm with the highest
posterior mean is the true best and the shortfall of that arm's value
below the best. Its last column is the total-variation distance
between each policy's action distribution and the exact vector at
mid-horizon, along that policy's own trajectory.

Thompson sampling and exact matching have the same expected regret by
Lemma~8, and their sample means differ by one to two percent of
cumulative regret in both regimes. The VBOS policy's mean regret is
ten to eleven percent above Thompson sampling's, and its action
distribution is at total-variation distance $0.13$ to $0.16$ from the
computed vector. F-LITE's is four to five percent above. Per-replication
regrets were not saved, so these differences carry no paired
intervals.

The identification rates do not separate the policies. Their
differences are within two binomial standard errors at 100
replications. The regret and distance columns carry the comparison.

\section{Scope}
\label{sec:limits}

The derivations of Section~\ref{sec:posteriors} are exact because the
observations there are generated with factor structure. When scenario
effects enter a simulation nonlinearly, a low-rank model of $\Lambda$
is an approximation, and the posterior inherits its error. When a
Gaussian-process kernel is dense with a short length scale, a factor
fit has to precede the computation, and its residual is the cost.

The probability of optimality is not the knowledge gradient. Under
simple-regret loss, the one-step value of a measurement is the
knowledge-gradient quantity \citep{frazier2008}, an expectation over
the next posterior means. \citet{tuisov2026} shows that this is the
normative myopic value of computation, and notes that the exact
dynamic version ``is itself generally intractable.'' Neither quantity
is computed here.

The regret analysis of \citet{odonoghue2021} depends on the VBOS
tilt. Its measured distance from $p$ quantifies the approximation, but
does not by itself make a case against the tilt.

Thompson sampling needs one posterior sample and no vector. The exact
vector matters where a sample is not enough: stopping, allocation,
constraints and derandomization. Both experiments above are of that
kind.
