\title{\textbf{A Posterior Predictive for Pass@$k$}\\[0.4em]
\large Established Empirical Bayes, Measured on Released Rollouts}
\author{Peter Cotton}
\date{\normalsize First version: September 2, 2026. This version: November 17, 2026.}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent Pass@$k$ is often extrapolated from a few scored attempts
per prompt by the plug-in $1-(1-\hat p)^k$, which assigns zero to
every prompt that has not yet succeeded. \citet{chen2021} noted the
bias, \citet{kazdan2025} corrected the aggregate with a fitted
difficulty prior, and \citet{verine2026} published the per-prompt
posterior predictive. We evaluate that estimator on nine thousand
prompts of released rollouts, with two training attempts per prompt
and six held out. The plug-in understates aggregate pass@6 by
seventeen points, and a third of the $5{,}511$ prompts it rates
unreachable succeed in the held-out attempts. Shrinking the point
estimate accounts for most of the log-loss improvement, integrating
over the posterior adds a smaller further gain, and a fixed Jeffreys
prior obtains nearly all the gain of the fitted priors.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Language models are improved after pretraining by having them
attempt problems that are scored automatically: a unit test passes,
a final answer matches, a verifier accepts. Evaluations sample each
prompt several times, because a model that solves a problem once in
sixteen tries can often be trained to solve it reliably. Pass@$k$ is
the probability that at least one of $k$ sampled attempts succeeds,
averaged over prompts. It is used to decide which prompts to train
on, whether a fine-tuning recipe helped, and when to stop sampling.

When $n \ge k$ scored attempts per prompt are available, the
combinatorial estimator of \citet{chen2021},
$1-\binom{n-c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}$ with $c$ the number of correct
attempts, is unbiased for the per-prompt probability. It applies
only when $k \le n$. An unbiased estimator is also not necessarily
the best predictor under log loss or Brier score, which is how
predictions are scored below.

A pass@$k$ value is often wanted at a depth $k$ larger than the
number of attempts scored. A common device is to estimate the
per-attempt success rate by $\hat p = s/m$, from $s$ successes in
$m$ attempts, and report $1-(1-\hat p)^k$. \citet{verine2026} call
this the naive plug-in estimator. We call it the plug-in.

\citet{malladi2026} use the same transformation inside a coverage
diagnostic: ``we convert pass@1 to an estimated pass@16 value using
$f_{16}(p):=1-(1-p)^{16}$.'' Their pass@1 estimates rest on at least
sixteen samples per problem per evaluation seed, across three seeds
(their Appendix D.3). They therefore apply the transformation at
substantial sampling depth and do not extrapolate beyond it. The
bias of Proposition~\ref{prop:bias} has the same sign in their
setting, but the magnitudes measured here do not carry over to it.

\citet{chen2021} already cautioned that the plug-in is biased.
\citet{kazdan2025} correct the aggregate: they fit a beta prior over
prompt difficulty by marginal maximum likelihood and report the
prior expectation of pass@$k$. A prompt with no successes then
contributes through the prior and is not assigned zero.

\citet{verine2026} publish the per-prompt version: ``we therefore
use the posterior predictive pass@$k$ for each task and average
across tasks.'' They derive the estimator as a ratio of beta
functions, add zero-one inflated priors, and prove a theorem
ordering population pass@$k$ at fixed pass@1. Public evaluation code
also implements the beta-binomial version.

The estimator is theirs, and the evaluation is ours. The data are
new, the design extrapolates from two attempts to six, and
predictions are scored per prompt against held-out attempts. We
measure the size of the plug-in's errors, how much of the
improvement comes from shrinkage and how much from integrating over
the posterior, whether a fixed prior suffices, how well the fitted
models are calibrated, and how a threshold decision changes. A
probit-normal prior is run alongside the beta and performs
comparably.

\section{Related work}

\citet{brown2024} established repeated sampling as an axis of
inference-time scaling. They model aggregate coverage curves with
exponentiated power laws and compute per-problem pass rates with the
combinatorial estimator. \citet{schaeffer2025} explain those power
laws by the heavy-tailed distribution of per-problem success
probabilities. They fit a three-parameter Kumaraswamy distribution
to per-problem point estimates and forecast coverage by evaluating
the integral that defines aggregate pass@$k$.

\citet{kazdan2025} fit a beta difficulty prior by marginal maximum
likelihood, report aggregate coverage as the prior expectation of
$1-(1-p)^k$, and apply it to budget allocation.

\citet{verine2026} supply the estimator evaluated here. Counts are
binomial under a shared beta difficulty prior fitted by marginal
maximum likelihood. The posterior is updated with each task's count,
and the posterior expectation of future pass@$k$ is computed per
task and averaged. Their beta-function ratio is algebraically the
rising-factorial ratio~\eqref{eq:rising}. They distinguish the prior
expectation of coverage from the average of posterior predictions,
as we do after Proposition~\ref{prop:bayes}. They also provide
zero-one inflated priors and an ordering theorem for population
pass@$k$ at fixed pass@1.

\citet{yang2026} use the same held-out design to ask whether a
post-training crossover in pass@$k$ is statistically established.
They fit difficulty heterogeneity on one half of a model's
generations and score it by the posterior-predictive likelihood of
the other half on the same prompts. Their confidence bands stay
within $k \le n$, where the unbiased estimator exists. Beyond that
range they adopt the fit of \citet{kazdan2025}, so the plug-in
defect measured here enters their extrapolation stage and not their
bands.

\citet{hariri2025} replace pass@$k$ reporting with Bayesian credible
intervals at the model level, and \citet{miller2024} bring
frequentist standard errors to evaluation reporting. The diagnostic
of \citet{malladi2026} is the motivating example. We do not dispute
their method. We examine only the extrapolation step inside it.

\section{The estimation problem}

There are $N$ prompts and a compute budget for $m$ scored attempts
at each. The data are success counts $s_1, \dots, s_N$ with
$s_i \in \{0, \dots, m\}$. Decisions concern a larger budget $k > m$
that has not been spent. Whether a prompt is worth training on
depends on its chance of being solved within $k$ attempts, and
whether a fine-tuning method helped depends on how many prompts
exceed a threshold on that chance.

The task is to choose a function of $s_i$ to stand in for the deeper
measurement. We judge a choice as a prediction: per prompt by log
loss and Brier score against later attempts, and in aggregate by the
error of the reported pass@$k$ curve.

The model is as follows. Each prompt has a latent per-attempt
success probability $p_i$, drawn independently from a difficulty
distribution $G$ on $[0,1]$. Given $p_i$, attempts are independent
$\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_i)$ trials, so $s_i \sim \mathrm{Bin}(m,
p_i)$.

There are two estimands. The first is the per-prompt probability
that a fresh batch of $k$ attempts contains a success,
\begin{equation}
q_k(p_i) \;=\; 1-(1-p_i)^{k},
\end{equation}
which must be predicted from $s_i$ alone. The second is the
aggregate pass@$k$ of the population,
\begin{equation}
\pi_k \;=\; \int_0^1 \bigl(1-(1-p)^k\bigr)\,dG(p).
\end{equation}

Attempts are drawn with independent seeds, so we accept conditional
independence given $p_i$. The difficulty $p_i$ varies across
prompts, and $s_i$ is a noisy measurement of it.

\section{The plug-in and its failure mode}

At $s_i=0$ the plug-in predicts zero at every $k$, whatever the
value of $m$. Each later success on such a prompt then incurs
unbounded log loss. The same happens at $s_i = m$, where the plug-in
predicts one, for prompts whose later attempts all fail.
\citet{chen2021} noted the aggregate bias.
Proposition~\ref{prop:bias} adds strictness and the $k=1$ boundary
case.

\begin{proposition}[The plug-in is biased downward for every
difficulty]\label{prop:bias}
Fix $m \ge 1$ and $k \ge 2$, and let $s \sim \mathrm{Bin}(m,p)$.
Then
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\,1-\bigl(1-\tfrac{s}{m}\bigr)^{k}\Bigr]
\;\le\; 1-(1-p)^k,
\end{equation}
with equality only at $p \in \{0,1\}$; at $k=1$ equality holds for
all $p$. Consequently the expected aggregate plug-in curve lies
below $\pi_k$ for every $G$ not concentrated on $\{0,1\}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $\varphi(x) = (1-x)^k$ on $[0,1]$. Its second derivative is
$\varphi''(x) = k(k-1)(1-x)^{k-2} \ge 0$, strictly positive on
$[0,1)$ when $k \ge 2$, so $\varphi$ is convex, and strictly convex
there. The estimate $\hat p = s/m$ satisfies
$\mathbb{E}\,\hat p = p$. Jensen's inequality gives
$\mathbb{E}\,\varphi(\hat p) \ge \varphi(\mathbb{E}\,\hat p)
= (1-p)^k$, and the inequality is strict whenever $\hat p$ is
nondegenerate, which is exactly $p \in (0,1)$. Subtracting both
sides from one gives the display.

At $k=1$, $\varphi$ is
affine and Jensen holds with equality. The aggregate statement
follows by integrating the pointwise inequality against $G$ and
taking expectations over the counts.
\end{proof}

The sign of the bias is the same for every $p$, but its size is not
monotone in $k$. At $m=2$ and $p=0.9$ the bias is $0.0315$ at $k=3$
and $0.0212$ at $k=4$. The bias shrinks at large $k$ because
$q_k(p)$ approaches one. In the experiments below the bias grows
over the displayed range of $k$. That reflects the difficulty
distribution of this data and is not a consequence of convexity.

\section{The posterior predictive}

Give $p$ a two-parameter prior, fit the parameters by marginal
maximum likelihood across prompts, and predict with the posterior
expectation
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{pass}}_k^{\,\mathrm{post}}
\;=\; \mathbb{E}\bigl[\,1-(1-p)^k \,\bigm|\, s\,\bigr].
\label{eq:post}
\end{equation}
Under a $\mathrm{Beta}(a,b)$ prior the posterior is
$\mathrm{Beta}(a+s,\; b+m-s)$ and
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\bigl[(1-p)^k \mid s\bigr]
= \prod_{j=0}^{k-1} \frac{b+m-s+j}{a+b+m+j},
\label{eq:rising}
\end{equation}
a ratio of rising factorials, so Equation~\eqref{eq:post} has a
closed form.

Under a probit-normal prior, $p = \Phi(\theta)$ with
$\theta \sim N(\mu, \tau^2)$, the marginal likelihood and the
posterior moment are one-dimensional Gauss--Hermite quadratures over
$\theta$. We include this family because a factor-correlated
portfolio of successes takes this form after conditioning
\citep{cotton2026}.

Because $q_k$ is concave, the posterior predictive is no larger than
the plug-in evaluated at the posterior mean:
$\mathbb{E}[q_k(p) \mid s] \le q_k(\mathbb{E}[p \mid s])$. This
gives three predictors to compare. Moving from the plug-in at $s/m$
to the plug-in at the posterior mean is the effect of shrinking the
point estimate toward the population. Moving from the plug-in at the
posterior mean to the posterior predictive is the effect of
integrating $q_k$ over the posterior. Table~\ref{tab:perprompt}
reports the two effects separately.

\begin{proposition}[Optimality of the posterior predictive under
the true prior]
\label{prop:bayes}
Let $Y$ indicate a success among $k$ fresh attempts at the same
prompt, drawn independently of the training attempts given $p$.
Under the model, with prior equal to the true difficulty law $G$:
(i) $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid s] =
\mathbb{E}[\,1-(1-p)^k \mid s\,]$, the exact conditional success
probability; (ii) it minimizes expected Brier score and expected log
loss among all predictors that depend on the data only through $s$;
(iii) its population expectation equals $\pi_k$:
$\mathbb{E}_{s}\bigl[\mathbb{E}[q_k(p) \mid s]\bigr] = \pi_k$ when
the outer expectation is taken under the model's marginal law of
$s$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Given $p$, $Y$ is Bernoulli with parameter $q_k(p)$ and is
independent of $s$. The tower property gives
$\mathbb{E}[Y \mid s]
= \mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}[Y \mid p, s] \bigm| s\bigr]
= \mathbb{E}[q_k(p) \mid s]$, which is (i). For (ii), the
conditional expectation minimizes mean squared error among
$s$-measurable predictors, which is the Brier statement, and the
true conditional probability minimizes expected log loss because the
excess log loss of any other predictor is the expectation of a
Kullback--Leibler divergence, which is nonnegative. For (iii),
$\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}[q_k(p) \mid s]\bigr]
= \mathbb{E}\,q_k(p) = \pi_k$, the tower property again.
\end{proof}

These are standard facts of Bayesian prediction and are not a
contribution. Part (iii) concerns population expectations under the
true model. In a fitted sample, the average of posterior predictions
need not equal the prior expectation of coverage under the fitted
prior, which is the population estimator of \citet{kazdan2025}. The
tables below therefore report both.

Here the two nearly coincide, because a two-parameter family fitted
to counts from $m=2$ attempts nearly interpolates the three-cell
histogram of counts. That agreement is specific to this design.

\section{Numerical study on released rollouts}

The data are the publicly released Pass8 rollout records of the RLVE
training suite.\footnote{Dataset:
\texttt{CL-From-Nothing/RLVE-Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507-Pass8-Rollouts}
on Hugging Face. Extraction and experiment scripts are committed
under \texttt{research/cavity\_calculus/exp2\_passk/} in the
\texttt{winning} repository.} The tasks are procedurally generated
reasoning problems, each with an automatic verifier, of the kind
used as reinforcement-learning environments for language models.
There are nine thousand prompts across eighteen environment
families, each with eight scored samples from the Qwen3-4B-Thinking
model.

RLVE rewards include partial credit: of $23{,}152$ positive rewards,
$2{,}988$ lie strictly between zero and one. We define success as a
reward of at least one, since the released rows carry no separate
accuracy field. Defining success as any positive reward changes the
numbers in every table but none of the conclusions.

Samples $0$--$1$ are the training attempts ($m=2$) and samples
$2$--$7$ are held out. The per-prompt target is whether any of the
six held-out samples succeeds ($k = 6 > m$). It is a single
Bernoulli outcome, scored by log loss and Brier score. The aggregate
reference for $k \le 6$ is the combinatorial estimator applied to
the six held-out samples only. It is an estimate, not the truth. The
fitted priors are $\mathrm{Beta}(0.31, 0.79)$ and probit-normal with
$\mu = -1.06$, $\tau = 1.53$.

Table~\ref{tab:perprompt} separates the two effects. Shrinkage alone
(row two) removes most of the plug-in's log loss. Integrating over
the posterior improves on row two by a further eleven percent in log
loss and seven percent in Brier score. The Jeffreys prior, which has
no fitted parameters, obtains nearly all the gain of the fitted
priors.

In aggregate (Table~\ref{tab:aggregate}) the plug-in is $17.4$
points below the reference at $k=6$, and the posterior rows are
within $1.3$ points of it. The two posterior rows agree to three
decimals for the reason given after Proposition~\ref{prop:bayes}.

Calibration can be checked by training count. For the $5{,}511$
prompts with no training success, the fitted beta predicts a
held-out success rate of $0.318$ and the observed rate is $0.343$.
At counts one and two the predictions are $0.843$ and $0.982$
against observed rates of $0.834$ and $0.977$. The plug-in predicts
$0.984$ and $1.000$ at those counts.

With a common prior and common $m$, every prompt with the same count
receives the same prediction. Both methods therefore rank prompts by
count. The posterior predictive changes the predicted probabilities,
and any decision made by thresholding them, but not the order of
prompts.

The coverage diagnostic of \citet{malladi2026} works on a
base-reachable set: prompts whose estimated pass@16 lies in
$[0.05, 0.95]$. We apply the lower edge, $0.05$, as a reachability
screen on predicted pass@6. The plug-in rates the $5{,}511$
zero-count prompts unreachable, and $1{,}890$ of them, a third,
succeed within the six held-out attempts. The posterior predictive
assigns those prompts $0.318$, close to their observed rate of
$0.343$, and rates none unreachable.

At this sampling depth, a third of the prompts discarded by the
plug-in screen were solved within six attempts. The
posterior-predictive screen discards nothing, which is the correct
decision when the predicted probability is near one third and the
threshold is $0.05$.

\section{Scope}

The bias and its correction are prior work. The contribution is the
set of measurements, which hold for the setting studied.

The magnitudes depend on the noise in $\hat p$. Two training
attempts is an extreme case, chosen so that $k=6$ is an
extrapolation. \citet{malladi2026} use sixteen or more samples per
problem per seed, where the errors caused by noise are far smaller.

The data cover one model on one environment suite. Success is
defined by full reward because the data have no separate accuracy
field.

The two fitted families agree closely, but agreement at $m=2$ says
little about the difficulty distribution. Neither family places an
atom at $p=0$, so both predict that every prompt eventually succeeds
as $k$ grows. Two attempts cannot identify the tail behavior needed
to extrapolate to arbitrary $k$. The zero-one inflated priors of
\citet{verine2026} address this.

The study supports three statements at small sampling depth.
Shrinkage of any reasonable kind removes most of the plug-in's
per-prompt loss. Integrating over the posterior adds a smaller,
measured improvement. Threshold decisions of the kind diagnostics
make change materially.

Two extensions are left for future work. The first is to measure the
effect of the substitution inside a diagnostic at its native
sampling depth on math and code benchmarks. The second is to treat
correlated, heterogeneous portfolios of attempts. The probit-normal
family combines with factor structure \citep{cotton2026}, but we
demonstrate nothing of that kind here.
