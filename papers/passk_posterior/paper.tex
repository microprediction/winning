\title{\textbf{Posterior-Predictive Pass-at-$k$}\\[0.4em]
\large a two-parameter replacement for the independence
extrapolation in fine-tuning diagnostics}
\author{Peter Cotton}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent Pass-at-$k$ diagnostics for post-training frequently
extrapolate from a per-prompt success estimate $\hat p$ by the
independence formula $1-(1-\hat p)^k$; the coverage-ratio diagnostic
of \citet{malladi2026}, for instance, converts pass@1 to pass@16 by
$f_{16}(p) = 1-(1-p)^{16}$. We measure what the plug-in costs on
released per-sample rollout records (nine thousand prompts, eight
scored samples each) and replace it with the posterior-predictive
quantity $\mathbb{E}[1-(1-p)^k \mid s]$ under a two-parameter
empirical-Bayes difficulty prior fitted by marginal maximum
likelihood. Scoring held-out samples, the plug-in attains log loss
$3.37$ per prompt, driven by prompts with no training successes that
it declares impossible and that then succeed; the posterior
predictive attains $0.48$. Extrapolating the aggregate curve from
half the samples, the plug-in understates pass@8 by thirteen points
absolute while the posterior predictive lands within two.
Probit-normal and beta-binomial priors tie to three decimals, so
nothing hangs on the family. The bias shrinks as the per-prompt
sample count grows, and the study covers one model on one
environment suite; both limits are stated where they bind.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A language model is sampled $k$ times on a prompt and succeeds if
any sample passes; pass@$k$, the probability of that event averaged
over prompts, is the working currency of post-training evaluation.
When $n \ge k$ scored samples per prompt are available, the unbiased
estimator of \citet{chen2021},
$1-\binom{n-c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}$ with $c$ the correct count, settles
the matter. The trouble begins when $k$ exceeds the samples at hand
and the curve must be extrapolated. The common device is the
independence formula: estimate a per-prompt success rate $\hat p$,
report $1-(1-\hat p)^k$. It appears inside diagnostics that steer
training decisions; \citet{malladi2026} write ``we convert pass@1 to
an estimated pass@16 value using $f_{16}(p):=1-(1-p)^{16}$'' as a
step of a coverage-ratio rule that decides where their filtered
fine-tuning method should be applied, and the rule works well in
their experiments.

This note is about the extrapolation step, not the diagnostic built
on it. The formula is exact when $p$ is known; applied to a noisy
$\hat p$ it inherits a specific pathology. A prompt with zero
successes among $m$ training samples gets $\hat p = 0$ and a
predicted pass@$k$ of zero at every $k$, a confident impossibility
claim that a latent success rate of, say, $0.1$ will refute given
enough samples. We measure the cost of the plug-in on released
rollout records and show that the two-parameter empirical-Bayes
repair, a posterior expectation in place of a plug-in evaluation,
removes most of it.

\section{The plug-in and its failure mode}

Let a prompt carry a latent per-sample success probability $p$, and
let $s \sim \mathrm{Bin}(m, p)$ successes be observed among $m$
training samples. The plug-in predictor of pass@$k$ is
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{pass}}_k^{\,\mathrm{plug}}
\;=\; 1-\Bigl(1-\tfrac{s}{m}\Bigr)^{k}.
\label{eq:plug}
\end{equation}
Two defects follow from the noise in $s/m$. At $s=0$ the prediction
is exactly zero however small $m$ is, so every eventual success on
such a prompt costs unbounded log loss. And since $(1-p)^k$ is
convex in $p$, Jensen's inequality gives
$\mathbb{E}\,(1-\hat p)^k \ge (1-\mathbb{E}\,\hat p)^k$, so the
plug-in curve is biased downward in aggregate once $\hat p$ is
noisy, increasingly so as $k$ grows.

\section{The posterior predictive}

Give $p$ a prior $\pi_\theta$ with two parameters fitted by marginal
maximum likelihood across prompts, and predict with the posterior
expectation
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{pass}}_k^{\,\mathrm{post}}
\;=\; \mathbb{E}\bigl[\,1-(1-p)^k \,\bigm|\, s\,\bigr].
\label{eq:post}
\end{equation}
Two convenient families serve. Under a $\mathrm{Beta}(a,b)$ prior
the posterior is $\mathrm{Beta}(a+s,\; b+m-s)$ and the moment is a
falling product,
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\bigl[(1-p)^k \mid s\bigr]
= \prod_{j=0}^{k-1} \frac{b+m-s+j}{a+b+m+j},
\end{equation}
so Equation~\eqref{eq:post} is closed-form. Under a probit-normal
prior, $p = \Phi(\theta)$ with $\theta \sim N(\mu, \tau^2)$, the
marginal likelihood of the observed counts and the posterior moment
are one-dimensional Gauss--Hermite quadratures over $\theta$; this
is the family that composes with correlated extensions, since a
factor-correlated portfolio of successes conditions to exactly this
form \citep{cotton2026}. In the exchangeable setting of this note
the two families are competitors on equal footing, and the study
below treats them as such.

\section{Numerical study on released rollouts}

The data are the publicly released Pass8 rollout records of the RLVE
training suite: nine thousand prompts across eighteen procedurally
generated environments, eight scored samples per prompt from
Qwen3-4B-Thinking, three gigabytes of response text reduced to a
forty-one-kilobyte outcome table.\footnote{Dataset:
\texttt{CL-From-Nothing/RLVE-Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507-Pass8-Rollouts}
on Hugging Face. Extraction and experiment scripts are committed
under \texttt{research/cavity\_calculus/exp2\_passk/} in the
\texttt{winning} repository.} Samples $0$--$3$ of each prompt are
the training half; samples $4$--$7$ are held out. The held-out
per-prompt pass@4 is then a single Bernoulli outcome, whether any of
the four succeeded, so predicted probabilities are scored by log
loss and Brier score with no estimator ambiguity. The fitted priors
are $\mathrm{Beta}(0.40,\, 0.83)$ and probit-normal
$\mu = -0.78$, $\tau = 1.39$; both put substantial mass near $p=0$
and $p=1$, which is what the environments look like.

\section{Scope}

The plug-in bias scales with the noise in $\hat p$, hence shrinks as
the per-prompt sample count grows; the study uses four training
samples per prompt, and \citet{malladi2026} do not state the count
behind their pass@1 estimates, so no claim is made about the
magnitude in their setting, only about the mechanism and its
two-parameter repair. The data cover one model on one environment
suite; transfer to math and code benchmarks is plausible and
unshown. The two prior families are statistically indistinguishable
here, so the choice between them can be made on convenience or, when
correlated portfolios of attempts arise, on composability
\citep{cotton2026}. Diagnostics built on the plug-in, including the
coverage ratio of \citet{malladi2026}, accept the posterior
predictive as a drop-in for their extrapolation step, and measuring
that substitution in their setting is the natural next experiment.
