\title{\textbf{A Posterior Predictive for Pass@$k$}\\[0.4em]
\large Per-Prompt Coverage Estimation for Post-Training Diagnostics}
\author{Peter Cotton}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent A prompt that fails a handful of scored attempts is rated
impossible by the extrapolation $1-(1-\hat p)^k$, and rated so at
every $k$. The bias of this plug-in rule has been known since
\citet{chen2021} flagged it, and the population-level repair, a
two-parameter difficulty prior fitted by marginal maximum
likelihood, has recently been published by \citet{kazdan2025} for
aggregate coverage curves. What remains open, and what post-training
diagnostics actually need, is the per-prompt version: the posterior
expectation $\mathbb{E}[1-(1-p)^k \mid s]$, which conditions on each
prompt's own count. We prove it is the exact conditional success
probability under the model, optimal under Brier and log loss, and
aggregate-consistent with the published population estimator. On
nine thousand prompts with eight scored samples each, the plug-in
scores held-out log loss $3.37$, driven entirely by zero-success
prompts that then succeed; the posterior predictive scores $0.48$
and lands the extrapolated aggregate curve within two points where
the plug-in is thirteen under. Probit-normal and beta-binomial
priors tie, so nothing hangs on the family.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Language models are improved after pretraining by having them
attempt problems and scoring the attempts automatically: a unit test
passes, a final answer matches, a verifier accepts. The evaluations
that steer this process sample each prompt many times, because a
model that solves a problem once in sixteen tries can often be
trained into solving it reliably. Pass@$k$, the probability that at
least one of $k$ sampled attempts succeeds, averaged over prompts,
is the working currency of post-training evaluation, and decisions
of real consequence ride on it: which prompts to train on, whether a
fine-tuning recipe helped, when to stop sampling.

When $n \ge k$ scored attempts per prompt are available, the
combinatorial estimator of \citet{chen2021},
$1-\binom{n-c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}$ with $c$ the correct count, is
unbiased and settles the matter. The trouble begins when $k$ exceeds
the attempts at hand and the curve must be extrapolated. The common
device evaluates the coverage formula at a point estimate: from
$\hat p = s/m$, report $1-(1-\hat p)^k$. We will call this the
plug-in. It is not a straw man. \citet{malladi2026} write ``we
convert pass@1 to an estimated pass@16 value using
$f_{16}(p):=1-(1-p)^{16}$'' inside the coverage diagnostic that
decides where their filtered fine-tuning method applies;
\citet{barakat2026} build a pass@$k$ optimization analysis on the
same per-prompt functional; and every pass@$k$ curve reported beyond
its sampled depth uses some version of it. \citet{chen2021} already
cautioned that the plug-in is biased, and their combinatorial
estimator repairs the interpolation regime $k \le n$; it offers
nothing for extrapolation, which is where the diagnostics live.

This note supplies and prices the per-prompt repair. The plug-in has
two defects that a two-parameter model removes: it is biased
downward for every prompt difficulty, by convexity, and it assigns
probability exactly zero to any prompt with no observed successes,
an impossibility verdict that later attempts routinely refute. The
repair replaces evaluation at a point estimate with a posterior
expectation, $\mathbb{E}[1-(1-p)^k \mid s]$, under a difficulty
prior fitted once across prompts by marginal maximum likelihood. At
the population level this idea has just been published:
\citet{kazdan2025} fit a beta prior by marginal likelihood and
report the prior expectation of coverage, obtaining well-calibrated
aggregate pass@$k$ curves. The per-prompt object is the missing
piece. Diagnostics act prompt by prompt, the zero-success pathology
is a per-prompt pathology, and conditioning on each prompt's own
count is what a posterior is for. We prove the conditional version
is the exact per-prompt success probability under the model, optimal
under both proper scoring rules in play, and aggregate-consistent
with the published population estimator, then measure all of it on
released rollout records.

\section{Related work}

\citet{brown2024} established repeated sampling as an inference-time
scaling axis and model aggregate coverage curves with exponentiated
power laws, computing per-problem pass rates by the combinatorial
estimator. \citet{schaeffer2025} explain those power laws by the
heavy-tailed distribution of per-problem success probabilities,
fitting a three-parameter Kumaraswamy law to plug-in per-problem
estimates and forecasting coverage through the same integral that
defines aggregate pass@$k$; their per-problem inputs are point
estimates, not posteriors. \citet{kazdan2025} are closest to this
note: a beta difficulty prior fitted by marginal maximum likelihood,
aggregate coverage reported as the prior expectation of
$1-(1-p)^k$, with zero-success problems handled by the prior mass
rather than declared impossible, and a budget-allocation
application. They never condition on an individual prompt's count;
every prompt receives the same curve. The present note is the
conditional complement: the posterior predictive per prompt, its
optimality, and its measured advantage where diagnostics operate.
Elsewhere in the evaluation literature, \citet{hariri2025} replace
pass@$k$ reporting with Bayesian credible intervals at the model
level, and \citet{miller2024} bring frequentist standard errors to
eval reporting; neither extrapolates per-prompt coverage.
\citet{malladi2026} supply the motivating diagnostic, and nothing
here disputes their method: the question is confined to the
extrapolation step inside it.

\section{The estimation problem}

The practitioner's situation is this. There are $N$ prompts and a
compute budget that allows $m$ scored attempts at each, so the data
are success counts $s_1, \dots, s_N$ with $s_i \in \{0, \dots, m\}$.
Decisions are then taken about a larger budget $k > m$ that will not
be spent yet, or ever: whether a prompt is worth training on depends
on its chance of being solved within $k$ attempts, and whether a
fine-tuning method helped depends on how many prompts cross that
bar. \citet{malladi2026} instantiate the situation with $k = 16$
extrapolated from pass@1 estimates; any pass@$k$ curve reported
beyond its sampled depth instantiates it too. The problem is to
choose the function of $s_i$ that stands in for the unavailable
deeper measurement, and the quality of a choice is measured the way
predictions are: per prompt by log loss and Brier score against
later attempts, in aggregate by the error of the reported curve.

The model under which the problem has an exact answer is minimal.
Each prompt carries a latent per-attempt success probability $p_i$,
drawn independently from a difficulty distribution $G$ on $[0,1]$;
conditional on $p_i$, attempts are independent
$\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_i)$ trials, so $s_i \sim \mathrm{Bin}(m,
p_i)$. Two estimands follow. Per prompt, the probability that a
fresh batch of $k$ attempts contains a success,
\begin{equation}
q_k(p_i) \;=\; 1-(1-p_i)^{k},
\end{equation}
to be predicted from $s_i$ alone; and in aggregate, the pass@$k$ the
whole population would exhibit,
\begin{equation}
\pi_k \;=\; \int_0^1 \bigl(1-(1-p)^k\bigr)\,dG(p).
\end{equation}
The conditional independence of attempts given $p_i$ is not the
assumption under attack: attempts are drawn with independent seeds,
and the model grants that. What varies across the field is $p_i$
itself, and the whole question is how to respect that variation when
$s_i$ is a noisy glimpse of it. Evaluating $q_k$ at $s_i/m$ is one
answer; taking the posterior expectation of $q_k$ is the other; the
next two sections price them.

\section{The plug-in and its failure mode}

The plug-in predictor evaluates $q_k$ at the point estimate
$\hat p_i = s_i/m$:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{pass}}_k^{\,\mathrm{plug}}
\;=\; 1-\Bigl(1-\tfrac{s_i}{m}\Bigr)^{k}.
\label{eq:plug}
\end{equation}
At $s_i=0$ the prediction is exactly zero at every $k$, however
small $m$ is, so each eventual success on such a prompt costs
unbounded log loss. The aggregate defect was flagged by
\citet{chen2021}; the following restates it with the strictness and
the $k=1$ boundary that the experiments confirm.

\begin{proposition}[The plug-in is biased downward for every
difficulty]\label{prop:bias}
Fix $m \ge 1$ and $k \ge 2$, and let $s \sim \mathrm{Bin}(m,p)$.
Then
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\,1-\bigl(1-\tfrac{s}{m}\bigr)^{k}\Bigr]
\;\le\; 1-(1-p)^k,
\end{equation}
with equality only at $p \in \{0,1\}$; at $k=1$ equality holds for
all $p$. Consequently the aggregate plug-in curve lies below $\pi_k$
for every $G$ not concentrated on $\{0,1\}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $\varphi(x) = (1-x)^k$ on $[0,1]$. Its second derivative is
$\varphi''(x) = k(k-1)(1-x)^{k-2} \ge 0$, strictly positive on
$[0,1)$ when $k \ge 2$, so $\varphi$ is convex, and strictly convex
there. The estimate $\hat p = s/m$ satisfies
$\mathbb{E}\,\hat p = p$. Jensen's inequality gives
$\mathbb{E}\,\varphi(\hat p) \ge \varphi(\mathbb{E}\,\hat p)
= (1-p)^k$, and the inequality is strict whenever $\hat p$ is
nondegenerate, which is exactly $p \in (0,1)$. Negating and adding
one reverses the inequality into the display. At $k=1$, $\varphi$ is
affine and Jensen holds with equality. The aggregate statement
follows by integrating the pointwise inequality against $G$.
\end{proof}

The bias grows with the curvature of $\varphi$, hence with $k$, and
shrinks with the variance of $\hat p$, hence with $m$: extrapolating
further from fewer samples costs more, which is the regime
diagnostics live in.

\section{The posterior predictive}

Give $p$ a prior $\pi_\theta$ with two parameters fitted by marginal
maximum likelihood across prompts, and predict with the posterior
expectation
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{pass}}_k^{\,\mathrm{post}}
\;=\; \mathbb{E}\bigl[\,1-(1-p)^k \,\bigm|\, s\,\bigr].
\label{eq:post}
\end{equation}
Two convenient families serve. Under a $\mathrm{Beta}(a,b)$ prior
the posterior is $\mathrm{Beta}(a+s,\; b+m-s)$ and the moment is a
falling product,
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\bigl[(1-p)^k \mid s\bigr]
= \prod_{j=0}^{k-1} \frac{b+m-s+j}{a+b+m+j},
\end{equation}
so Equation~\eqref{eq:post} is closed-form. Under a probit-normal
prior, $p = \Phi(\theta)$ with $\theta \sim N(\mu, \tau^2)$, the
marginal likelihood of the observed counts and the posterior moment
are one-dimensional Gauss--Hermite quadratures over $\theta$; this
is the family that composes with correlated extensions, since a
factor-correlated portfolio of successes conditions to exactly this
form \citep{cotton2026}. In the exchangeable setting of this note
the two families are competitors on equal footing, and the study
below treats them as such.

\begin{proposition}[The posterior predictive is the target]
\label{prop:bayes}
Let $Y$ indicate a success among $k$ fresh attempts at the same
prompt, drawn independently of the training attempts given $p$.
Under the model, with prior equal to the true difficulty law $G$:
(i) $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid s] =
\mathbb{E}[\,1-(1-p)^k \mid s\,]$, so the posterior predictive is
the exact conditional success probability; (ii) it minimizes
expected Brier score and expected log loss among all predictors that
depend on the data only through $s$; (iii) its average over prompts
is exactly $\pi_k$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Given $p$, $Y$ is Bernoulli with parameter $q_k(p) = 1-(1-p)^k$ and
is independent of $s$. The tower property gives
$\mathbb{E}[Y \mid s]
= \mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}[Y \mid p, s] \bigm| s\bigr]
= \mathbb{E}[q_k(p) \mid s]$, which is (i). For (ii), the
conditional expectation minimizes mean squared error among
$s$-measurable predictors, which is the Brier statement, and the
true conditional probability minimizes expected log loss because the
excess log loss of any other predictor is the expectation of a
Kullback--Leibler divergence, which is nonnegative. For (iii),
$\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}[q_k(p) \mid s]\bigr]
= \mathbb{E}\,q_k(p) = \pi_k$, the tower property again.
\end{proof}

Part (iii) locates the published population estimator inside this
one: averaging the posterior predictive over prompts recovers the
prior expectation of coverage, which is precisely what
\citet{kazdan2025} report. Their estimator is the aggregate shadow
of Equation~\eqref{eq:post}; conditioning on $s_i$ is what the
posterior adds, and the per-prompt columns of the study below are
where the addition shows. Proposition~\ref{prop:bayes} holds at the
true $G$; in practice $G$ is replaced by a two-parameter family
fitted by marginal maximum likelihood, and the study measures what
survives that substitution.

\section{Numerical study on released rollouts}

The data are the publicly released Pass8 rollout records of the RLVE
training suite: procedurally generated reasoning tasks, each with an
automatic verifier, of the kind used as reinforcement-learning
environments for language models. They carry nine thousand prompts
across eighteen environment families, eight scored samples per
prompt from the Qwen3-4B-Thinking model, three gigabytes of response
text reduced to a forty-one-kilobyte outcome table.\footnote{Dataset:
\texttt{CL-From-Nothing/RLVE-Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507-Pass8-Rollouts}
on Hugging Face. Extraction and experiment scripts are committed
under \texttt{research/cavity\_calculus/exp2\_passk/} in the
\texttt{winning} repository.} Samples $0$--$3$ of each prompt are
the training half; samples $4$--$7$ are held out. The held-out
per-prompt pass@4 is then a single Bernoulli outcome, whether any of
the four succeeded, so predicted probabilities are scored by log
loss and Brier score with no estimator ambiguity. The fitted priors
are $\mathrm{Beta}(0.40,\, 0.83)$ and probit-normal $\mu = -0.78$,
$\tau = 1.39$; both put substantial mass near $p=0$ and $p=1$, which
is what the environments look like.

\section{Scope}

The population-level repair is prior art: \citet{kazdan2025} fit the
same beta family the same way and report well-calibrated aggregate
curves, and \citet{schaeffer2025} model the difficulty distribution
with a richer family for the same integral. The contribution here is
confined to the conditional object: the per-prompt posterior
predictive, its optimality under the scoring rules diagnostics use,
its aggregate consistency with the published estimator, and the
measured seven-fold per-prompt gap. The plug-in bias itself has been
on record since \citet{chen2021}; Proposition~\ref{prop:bias} adds
strictness, the $k=1$ boundary, and the aggregate consequence.

The bias scales with the noise in $\hat p$, hence shrinks as the
per-prompt sample count grows; the study uses four training samples
per prompt, and \citet{malladi2026} do not state the count behind
their pass@1 estimates, so the numbers here bound the mechanism, not
the magnitude in their setting. The data cover one model on one task
suite; transfer to standard math and code benchmarks is plausible
and unshown. The two prior families are statistically
indistinguishable here, so the choice between them can be made on
convenience or, when correlated portfolios of attempts arise, on
composability \citep{cotton2026}. Diagnostics built on the plug-in,
including the coverage ratio of \citet{malladi2026}, accept the
posterior predictive as a drop-in for their extrapolation step, and
measuring that substitution in their setting is the natural next
experiment.
