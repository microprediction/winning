\title{\textbf{Thurstone is the Model of Choice}\\[4pt]
\large\normalfont A century-old two-horse race, decided on archival human data}
\author{Peter Cotton}
\date{Provisional draft, 15 August 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent Two accounts of choice have competed since 1927. Luce's Choice Axiom
says that removing an alternative leaves the odds between the survivors
untouched. Thurstone's Gaussian race says the survivors re-race, and the odds
compress by a computable amount. Both are parameter-free, which makes the
comparison a race rather than a fitting exercise, and it was never decided on
evidence: a choice distribution must be estimated from repeated trials, and the
two predictions differ by less than the resulting standard errors on any single
menu. Archival data decide it. Writing the shift in log odds under restriction as
$-\lambda\log(p_i/p_j)$, the axiom requires $\lambda = 0$ and the race predicts a
specific positive value. Across eleven human datasets and some ninety thousand
respondents, spanning values, humour, food, film, perception, prestige, leisure
and multi-attribute consumer goods, the race is the closer prediction in eleven of
eleven, by a median factor of seven. Human choice structures are very nearly
random-utility representable and rank reversals are rare. Thurstone wins.
\end{abstract}

\section{A race nobody could run}

Choose among alternatives and something must govern how the probabilities move
when the set of alternatives changes. Luce's Choice Axiom \cite{luce1959} gives
one answer. Probability attaches to items in proportion to a fixed utility,
\begin{equation}
P(i \mid S) = \frac{u(i)}{\sum_{j \in S} u(j)},
\label{eq:luce}
\end{equation}
so removing an alternative rescales what remains and leaves every surviving odds
ratio exactly as it was. This is Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives, and it
is the assumption behind the logit, the softmax, and most applied discrete choice.

Thurstone \cite{thurstone1927} gives another. Each item $i$ carries a location
$a_i$ and a noisy performance $X_i \sim N(a_i, 1)$; the item chosen is the one
whose draw wins. Removing an alternative removes a competitor from a race, and the
survivors' odds compress, because a shorter field is easier to win and the
advantage of the favourite counts for less. One says rescale the urn, the other
says re-run the race.

The disagreement is old, sharp, and empirically undecided. It is sharp because the
two make different predictions about the same observable quantity, and because
Yellott \cite{yellott1977} showed the families are nested inside one framework:
Luce's axiom holds in a random utility contest if and only if the noise is double
exponential, so the argument is about the shape of noise and nothing else. It is
undecided because the quantity is hard to see. A choice distribution has to be
estimated from repeated trials, one categorical outcome at a time, and on a single
menu the two predictions sit within each other's standard errors. So the matter was
settled by tractability instead, and this paper argues that archival data settle it
properly, in Thurstone's favour, and not narrowly.

\section{Why the axiom won an argument it did not settle}
\label{sec:history}

The interesting historical question is not who was right but why one side
prevailed, because it did not prevail on evidence. It prevailed on arithmetic.

Thurstone's contest has no closed form beyond two alternatives. The probability
that one normal draw is extremal among $n$ is an $(n-1)$-dimensional integral with
no elementary expression. The equal-variance independent case that Mosteller
\cite{mosteller1951} made usable, universally called Case V and treated ever since
as though it were Thurstone's model rather than its most constrained member,
reduces the \emph{paired} comparison to $\Phi(d/\sqrt2)$ and no further. Everything
past a pair required numerical integration on machinery that did not exist. When
the econometric version was finally written down as multinomial probit
\cite{hausman1978} it was practical for a handful of alternatives at best, and
simulation made it estimable only decades later and never cheaply
\cite{train2009}.

The logistic alternative had the opposite property. Bradley and Terry
\cite{bradley1952} wrote a paired-comparison model with a closed form, a concave
log-likelihood, and estimation by hand. Luce \cite{luce1959} then supplied the
axiom, deriving Equation~\eqref{eq:luce} from Independence of Irrelevant
Alternatives, which converted a computational convenience into a behavioural
principle: the form was no longer merely tractable, it was implied. McFadden
\cite{mcfadden1974} made it the workhorse of applied discrete choice, and
Yellott's theorem completed the retrospective justification by identifying exactly
which noise law the axiom corresponds to, with the side effect of making the
double exponential look canonical rather than convenient.

The objections were immediate and they were always about restriction. Debreu
\cite{debreu1960}, reviewing Luce's book, observed that adding a near-duplicate
should divide that alternative's share rather than draw proportionally from
everything. Tversky \cite{tversky1972} showed that similarity violates simple
scalability altogether. Luce \cite{luce1977} reviewed the axiom's standing after
twenty years and treated its empirical failures as established. Yet the pattern of
the century was that the Luce form was repaired rather than replaced: nested logit,
the generalized extreme value family and mixed logit \cite{mcfaddentrain2000} were
all built to relax the independence IIA imposes while keeping the closed form that
made it estimable. Choosing the Thurstonian route instead meant paying the
integral, and almost nobody did.

Two things have changed. The integral is no longer expensive: the lattice method of
\cite{cotton2021} returns the whole vector of winning probabilities for fields of
$10^5$ entrants, so the tractability argument that decided the matter has expired.
And the measurement problem can be sidestepped entirely, by using data in which
respondents have already ranked everything.

\section{What the literature already knew, and what it did not}
\label{sec:lit}

That the axiom fails is not news. What has been missing is a comparison on equal
terms.

The failures were catalogued early and they were always failures of restriction.
Debreu's near-duplicate \cite{debreu1960}, Tversky's demonstration that similarity
violates simple scalability \cite{tversky1972}, the attraction effect of Huber,
Payne and Puto \cite{huber1982}, Simonson's compromise effect \cite{simonson1989},
and the context-dependent preferences of Tversky and Simonson
\cite{tverskysimonson1993} all show that what surrounds an alternative changes its
standing. Luce himself \cite{luce1977} treated the empirical case as closed.
Econometrics responded by relaxing independence while keeping the closed form,
through nested logit, the generalized extreme value family and mixed logit
\cite{mcfaddentrain2000}, with Hausman and McFadden \cite{hausman1984} supplying a
specification test for the violation.

Three features of that literature explain why the two-horse race stayed open.
First, most demonstrations are qualitative: they establish that a violation occurs,
often dramatically, without measuring how far the data sit from either contender.
Second, the influential paradigms use two- and three-item menus built from
constructed attribute profiles, which is ideal for showing an effect and poor for
estimating a law, because there is no larger field from which to restrict. Third,
the repairs are parameterised. Nested logit and mixed logit fit a covariance or a
mixing distribution, so their success does not tell us whether the unadorned
Gaussian race, which fits nothing, would have done as well.

The comparison made here is deliberately plainer. It pits the axiom against Case V
with no free parameters on either side, on population choice probabilities from
complete rankings rather than constructed menus, and scores both by distance from
what people did. What the older literature established is that the axiom is
violated. What has not been shown, so far as we can find, is that the 1927
alternative predicts the violation quantitatively, in domain after domain, without
being tuned.

Two threads bear directly on the design. Yellott's theorem \cite{yellott1977} is
what makes the race well posed rather than a matter of taste: since the axiom holds
in a random utility contest exactly when the noise is double exponential, the
contenders are one model with two noise laws, and a single statistic separates
them. And the Block--Marschak--Falmagne conditions \cite{block1960, falmagne1978}
let us ask the prior question of whether choice is representable by \emph{any}
random utility model, which Section~\ref{sec:human} does, so that the race is run
only where a race is possible.

\section{The statistic and its null}
\label{sec:statistic}

Complete rankings give both halves of the comparison at once. If a respondent has
ordered every alternative, their choice from any subset is their highest-ranked
available item, so the population's distribution over the full set and over any
pair are both computable without asking anyone a second question. Nothing is
imputed and no truncation model is needed.

For a pair $\{i,j\}$ write $p$ for the unrestricted population shares and $q$ for
the shares when the choice is narrowed to that pair. The shift
\begin{equation}
\delta_{ij} = \log\frac{q_i}{q_j} - \log\frac{p_i}{p_j}
\label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
is a ratio of ratios, so it is invariant to any rescaling of either distribution.
The axiom's prediction is not approximate: $\delta_{ij} = 0$. The race predicts a
specific negative value, obtained by calibrating locations $\{a_k\}$ to $p$ and
recomputing the winning probabilities of $i$ and $j$ alone.

It is convenient to summarize a dataset by the discount it applies to its own prior
odds,
\begin{equation}
\delta_{ij} = -\lambda \log\frac{p_i}{p_j},
\label{eq:lambda}
\end{equation}
fitted through the origin over all pairs. Then $\lambda = 0$ is the axiom,
$\lambda = 1$ discards the prior ranking entirely, and the race predicts an
intermediate value determined by the shape of $p$.

The ratio of the observed $\lambda$ to the race's prediction has an exact null.
If a population really is a Gaussian race at any noise scale, the ratio is one:
simulating populations at scales from $0.5$ to $3.0$ returns $1.00$ each time,
because calibrating locations to the observed distribution absorbs the scale.
The statistic therefore cannot be inflated by a population being noisier or
sharper than Case V, only by its being a different kind of process, and recovering
$1.00$ on simulated races is a positive control on the whole pipeline.

\section{Instruments, and two that fail}
\label{sec:instruments}

Not every source of rankings can carry this statistic, and the failures are
instructive enough to state before the results.

\textbf{Coarse rating scales cannot produce rankings.} Converting ratings to a
per-respondent order requires the respondent to use enough distinct values. On a
$0$ to $10$ scale with seven to nine items, they do not: in comparative election
studies only $0.3\%$ to $5\%$ of respondents produce a tie-free order over nine
parties, because people use about five or six of the eleven available levels. Only
genuinely continuous scales, or explicit rank-order tasks, avoid this.

\textbf{Preferential ballots are unreliable here.} Voters rank a few candidates and
leave the rest, which the data record as a final tied group, and the treatment of
that group swings the answer. Reading the tie as indifference gives $\lambda =
0.680$ across fifty-three files; counting only pairs a voter actually ordered gives
$0.147$. Worse, the instrument does not replicate: twelve APA presidential
elections, the same institution and the same five-candidate format with about ten
thousand ballots each, give ratios of $0.36$, $0.64$, $0.67$, $0.85$, $0.90$,
$1.04$, $1.60$, $1.87$, $1.99$, $2.03$, $2.06$ and $2.56$. A sevenfold spread
across replications of one design, far beyond sampling error at these sample sizes,
is what strategic ranking and self-selected truncation look like. Ballots are
excluded from what follows.

What remains are rank-order card tasks, continuous-scale ratings, and one
laboratory experiment with an explicit menu, all of which ask every respondent
about every alternative and give no reason to misreport.

\section{The race, scored}
\label{sec:scorecard}

Both contenders are parameter-free, which is what makes this a race rather than a
fitting exercise. Given an unrestricted distribution and nothing else, the axiom
says the survivors' odds are unchanged and the race says they compress by a
computed amount. Neither is tuned and neither sees a restricted distribution before
predicting one. A rule with a fitted parameter would not be in the race at all: a
temperature bolted onto Luce is a concession that the axiom has failed, not a
defence of it.

The verdict is not close in any domain. Observed discounts run from $0.19$ to
$0.59$ where the axiom requires zero, so the axiom is wrong by the whole of its
prediction every time, while the race is wrong by a few hundredths in the
best-elicited data. Three independent Jester samples of roughly twenty-four
thousand respondents each give $\lambda = 0.393$, $0.391$ and $0.374$, which is
the kind of agreement that says the measurement is reliable when the elicitation
is.

Where the race errs it errs in one direction: it under-predicts the discount. That
counts against the axiom rather than for it, since the true reallocation is even
further from renormalization than a Gaussian race allows. The largest margins come
from the cleanest instruments. The smallest, between $1.7$ and $2.2$, come from
multi-attribute goods and socially structured judgments, where context effects are
independently documented in the human literature \cite{huber1982, simonson1989};
even there the race wins.

\section{Order stability and random utility}
\label{sec:human}

A ranking cannot reverse: if a respondent prefers $i$ to $j$, that holds in every
subset containing both, by construction. So ranking data cannot test whether people
are order-stable when subset choices are elicited separately, and a laboratory
experiment is needed. Costa-Gomes and colleagues \cite{costagomes2022} had 373
subjects choose from \emph{every} subset of five real digital cameras, thirty-one
menus each, within subject.

Reversals are rare. Of $26{,}099$ nested comparisons in which the item chosen from
the larger menu was still available in the smaller one, $829$, or $3.2\%$, switched
away from it; among the forced-choice subgroup, who never defer and so require no
coding decisions, $417$ of $10{,}744$, or $3.9\%$. Pooling subjects gives population
choice probabilities for all thirty-one menus, which is what the
Block--Marschak--Falmagne conditions test \cite{block1960, falmagne1978}, and of
eighty computable sums exactly one falls below $-0.02$, the worst at $-0.055$.
Human choice over a fixed universe is very nearly representable by a random utility
model, which is the framework both contenders inhabit and which the axiom's failure
does not disturb.

\section{Discussion}

On the statistic that distinguishes them, the century-old race has a winner.
Luce's Choice Axiom is not a description of human choice under restriction: it is
wrong by the entirety of its prediction in every one of eleven datasets, across
values, humour, food, film, perception, prestige, leisure and consumer goods, and
in samples from one hundred and thirty to thirty-four thousand respondents.
Thurstone's race is the closer account everywhere, and in the best-elicited data it
is close in absolute terms as well.

Three qualifications belong in the same breath. The race systematically
under-predicts the discount, most in multi-attribute and socially structured
domains, so Case V is the right family and not the right member; a mildly skewed or
heavier-tailed performance density is the obvious next candidate and the calibration
machinery already supports it. Some datasets are small, with the camera and
occupational estimates resting on nine to fourteen pairs. And the whole comparison
concerns population choice probabilities under set restriction; nothing here
addresses within-person dynamics, learning, or choice over time.

For psychometrics the implication concerns defaults. The Bradley--Terry and
Plackett--Luce likelihoods that dominate paired-comparison and ranking analysis
inherit the axiom, and are chosen because they are tractable; Thurstonian
item-response and ranking models exist but are used far less. On the evidence here
that ordering is backwards. What the result mainly changes is a default. Renormalizing after removing an
option is the reflex of applied choice modelling, and it is the one prediction the
data reject in every domain examined. A parameter-free Gaussian race costs nothing
extra to compute now that the integral is cheap, and it is closer to what people do.

\section*{Data and code availability}

Every dataset used is public and free. Sushi rankings from
\cite{kamishima2003}; Jester ratings from the Eigentaste project
\cite{goldberg2001}, gauge-set files 1 to 3; the General Social Survey cumulative
file from NORC; perceptual, leisure, prestige, political-goal and election rankings
via PrefLib \cite{mattei2013} and the CRAN packages BayesMallows, ConsRank,
PerMallows and PLMIX; camera choices from the replication archive of
\cite{costagomes2022}. Analysis code, the derived tables, and the cached datasets
are in \texttt{research/human} of
\texttt{github.com/microprediction/winning}, together with the lattice
calibration used for the race predictions.

Two storage conventions must be distinguished when reproducing this: BayesMallows,
ConsRank and PerMallows record ranks, so element $j$ is the rank of alternative $j$,
while PLMIX records orderings, so the first element names the favourite. Confusing
them transposes the data silently. The conventions were established here by
cross-validation, since the German political-goals data ships in two packages and
the two agree exactly once each is read under its own convention.
